# Long-term variants

## Start from the business shape

Settled work often leaves two or more long-term business architectures open, for example one where the
number of main industries grows while a holding group of smaller businesses remains, and one where related
industries are integrated into a small number of segments, each carried by its own listed platform. For
each variant, write the business shape in one sentence, then derive the management architecture from it
rather than copying the near-term structure. Basis: structure follows the business logic of the variant,
what the industries share and how capital flows between them. Do not reuse the near-term structure
without deriving it; if the derivation gives the same structure, say why the variant's logic does not reach
it.

## Counting levels of control

A level of control is a layer that owns decisions and departments for the layer below. Count them per
variant:

- A layer whose units are all entities with their own departments adds a control level beneath it.
- A layer whose units are virtual (a head and a small team using the layer's departments) does not add
  a control level; the units are part of that layer's control.
- A group whose type evolves (from managing business units to managing industry segments) usually keeps
  the new segment layer virtual at first, because segment-level functions and scale have to be built up and
  an early entity layer duplicates platform departments; this does not hold when the segment is formed from
  an existing company with its own departments or the settled work gives it departments. Say whether it is
  still virtual in the variant you describe.

State the resulting count and check it against the entity or virtual status of every unit you list. A
variant that says "all units are virtual" and also lists departments inside those units is inconsistent.

## When units become entities or virtual

Decide entity or virtual status from the variant's business logic, not unit by unit in isolation. Ask what
the variant is built to share.

- Growth of separate main industries: a business that has been incubated successfully and runs at scale
  becomes its own business unit; the settled work usually gives the trigger. It is an entity, because the
  variant keeps industries separate and each owns its industry-specific capabilities. Where a level of
  entity business units already exists under that platform, existing entity units stay entities and the
  control count does not change; if the new unit is the first entity layer under a node that managed its
  businesses directly, the count rises by one.
- Integration of strongly related industries under one platform: the point of the integration is to run
  the capabilities those industries share (the ones the inputs describe as common to them, such as
  customer-facing or development functions) once, at the platform. Those functions move up to the platform
  headquarters, every business unit under that platform becomes virtual, and a unit that previously owned
  departments no longer has them, however large it is, because its size was the reason to share those
  functions, not a reason to keep them. Scope: a unit keeps the functions that regulation or a licence
  requires it to hold itself, and stays an entity with those only. Name the functions that move up; do not
  guess department names the inputs do not give.
- Industries that stay weakly related under a platform: there is little to share, so entity business units
  remain and the control count does not change.

Scope check: if the settled work states a different department arrangement for a variant, follow it and
say so. Test the reasoning by flipping the relatedness: a unit whose functions are shared in the
integrated variant should come out virtual there and an entity in the separate-industries variant; a unit
outside the integrated industries can keep the same status in both.

## Units the group still holds directly

In the near-term structure the top layer often still holds a few operating companies for reasons the
settled work states (a project in progress, an industry without a clear future). In a long-term variant,
apply those stated exit and transfer conditions: businesses that belong to a platform's industries move into
that platform; project companies end with their projects. Basis: a top layer under investment control holds
capital through platforms, so a direct operating holding needs a stated reason to remain; in a variant where
that reason has lapsed, the unit moves to a platform or exits. If the reason still holds, it stays. Say where
each held unit goes.

## Unrelated holdings

Integration rarely absorbs everything. Keep an explicit home for businesses unrelated to the integrated
industries and say how they are managed. Where the near-term structure already manages such businesses
through a portfolio unit under a platform (often virtual), keep that unit as their home in the variant and
say that it manages them as a portfolio. Basis: removing the home leaves those businesses without a
manager. Scope: if the settled work plans their exit or places them elsewhere, follow it.

## Headquarters departments

For each platform in each variant, list its departments grouped as vertical control, specialist, and
shared service where the inputs describe them or the variant changes them. Add a department only when a
function moved up or a new capability is needed in that variant, and name the function it carries. Do not
invent department lists for a platform whose departments the inputs never describe; say which functions it
needs in one sentence instead.

## Checks

1. Each variant states its business shape, its control-level count and the entity or virtual status of
   every business unit, and the three agree with the variant's business logic.
2. Every directly held unit has a destination.
3. Every function that left a business unit has a receiving platform department, or, where the inputs
   give no department names, a named receiving platform function.
4. Unrelated holdings have a named home.
5. Each variant lists its differences from the near-term architecture as decisions, not as description.
