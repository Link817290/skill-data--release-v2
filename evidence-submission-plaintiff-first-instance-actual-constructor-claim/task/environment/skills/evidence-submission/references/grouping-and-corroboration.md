# Grouping your own evidence and connecting the groups

Apply this whenever the deliverable presents your own client's evidence. It assumes you have built
the element map and the evidence inventory of the entry point.

## 1. Section order

1. Header, as in `filing-baseline.md` section 5: title, case number, addressee tribunal, and every
   party to the case (claimants, respondents, third parties) with its procedural status and, for an
   entity, domicile and legal representative where the inputs give them. Label the filing party,
   like every other, with the procedural status the instruction and the materials use for it.
2. General statement: what your client has to prove, which group carries which element, which group
   the others rest on and why (section 3), and the rule allocating the burden of proof, cited once.
3. The groups, in the source numbering. Each part names the group and its element, identifies its
   items by number and name, states the group proposition in your own prose, and ends with a short
   group conclusion.
4. Relationship between the groups: the corroboration pairs (section 6).
5. Burden of proof on the other side's defences (`burden-holding-and-request.md` section 1).
6. Request (`burden-holding-and-request.md` section 3).
7. Sign-off, with the filing party under the same designation as in the header and every
   authorised agent the inputs name.

Keep these section headings recognisable for what they are (general statement, group, relationship
between the groups, burden of proof, request). Do not insert further sections such as a separate
pricing section, a chronology, a chapter of conclusions ahead of the request, or a chapter restating
facts by issue; a pricing basis belongs in the group that proves the measure.

## 2. Group by what has to be proved

Each group carries one element from the element map: the legal relationship and its
characterisation, your client's status or capacity, performance, the measure, payments and the
balance, each respondent's liability, the defeat of a defence, the time bar. Where the evidence list
already groups the items, keep its groups and numbering and state the element each carries. Where
it does not, group by element and keep every item's own number. Never renumber.

## 3. The base group and the groups that depend on it

The groups are not equal. Usually one group establishes the fact that every other element
presupposes, and the other groups only matter if it holds.

- **Find it by dependency, not by difficulty.** For each group ask: if this group failed, which
  other groups would lose their subject? The base group is the one whose failure would leave the
  most other groups with nothing to attach to. A group that decides a hard legal characterisation
  is not the base group for that reason alone.
- **Write the dependency.** In the general statement, name the base group, the element it
  establishes, and the groups that rest on it, and say in one sentence how they rest on it (for
  example, in a claim for the price of goods sold, the groups on price and on late payment matter
  only once the group proving the contract and the delivery holds). In each dependent group's conclusion, refer back to the
  base group's element.
- **Order.** Where the source order lets you, the base group comes before its dependants. Where the
  source order is fixed, keep it and let the general statement carry the dependency.

Verification: a reader of the general statement alone can say which group everything rests on, and
which groups follow from it.

Counterexample: where two groups are genuinely independent (two separate claims joined in one case),
say that each carries its own claim; do not invent a hierarchy.

## 4. Write each group proposition as your own prose

For every group, state what the group proves: the element, the characterisation you ask the tribunal
to adopt, and the items it rests on. The stated proof purposes in the list are raw material, not
that statement. Close the group with a short conclusion in one or two sentences.

- Do not give admissibility verdicts on your own items. Stating that your own item is genuine,
  lawful and relevant adds nothing, and saying that a document you rely on is "not denied" or "not
  disputed" reads as a concession.
- Do not run a proves and does not prove analysis item by item. Mention an item individually only
  where it carries a point the group proposition does not.
- Where a figure or a pricing basis is the point of a group, give it with its source and derivation
  in that group (`figures-periods-and-limitation.md`).
- Describe the other side's conduct as the documents record it (it approved, it paid, it signed);
  do not convert it into knowledge or acceptance.

Verification: cover the list and read the group text alone. Can the reader state what the group
establishes?

## 5. Items in your bundle that someone else made

- A document the other side made (its letter, its account statement, its approval) is presented as
  that party's own record, binding on it: say what it records and what that establishes against its
  maker. Do not comment on its genuineness.
- A record kept by a public body or an independent third party is presented as such; say that it
  records the fact independently of either party.
- Where one of your client's own records carries a point, attach it to the counter signed, third
  party or public record that confirms it, rather than defending it on its own.

## 6. Corroboration across groups

Items in different groups often prove the same fact from different sources. The tribunal gives
weight to agreement only when the two items speak to the same dimension of the same fact.

**Operation.**

1. For each fact the case turns on, list its dimensions: who (the persons or entities), how much or
   how many (quantity, amount), when (dates, periods), what (the scope of work, goods or services).
2. For each dimension, find items in different groups that record it, preferably from different
   makers (one from your client and one from the other side, a third party or a public body).
3. Pair only items that record the same dimension. Write each pair in one sentence: the two items by
   number, the dimension, the two sources, and what agrees between them.
4. Give priority to the dimensions the other side will call one sided, and to the dimension on
   which a disputed figure or status rests.

Verification: each pair sentence names two items, one dimension and the agreement. A list of item
numbers without the dimension and the agreement is a cross reference, not an argument.

Counterexamples:

- Two items on different dimensions (a letter fixing a date and an invoice fixing an amount, a
  delivery note and a bank transfer for a different period) do not corroborate each other, even if
  both concern the same transaction.
- Two items made by the same party on the same occasion add little to each other; look for an
  independent maker.
- Do not pair every possible couple. A few pairs that carry contested elements are worth
  more than a table of all of them.

## 7. Stop conditions

- Do not reproduce the evidence list as argument; identify the items and argue in your own words.
- Do not rehearse what the other side may say about each item.
- If the source numbering is inconsistent, identify each item by number and name; do not renumber
  and do not add a note about it.
- No applications of any kind (production orders, collection of evidence, appraisal, verification,
  witness summons) unless the instruction calls for them.
