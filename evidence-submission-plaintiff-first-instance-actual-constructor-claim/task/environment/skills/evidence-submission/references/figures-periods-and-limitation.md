# Figures, measures, periods, and the time bar in an evidence submission

Apply this before writing any group proposition or request that turns on an amount, a pricing
basis, a payment, a set off, a count, a period, a time bar, or the order of events. These are the
sentences the other side attacks first, and the ones a tribunal copies into its findings when they
are right.

## 1. Choose the basis of the measure, and say why

Case files often contain several bases for the same measure: the contract price, a price in a
related contract between other parties, a valuation or appraisal, a settlement statement, a figure
the other side has itself acknowledged. They are rarely equal.

- List, in your notes, every competing basis with the document it comes from.
- Decide which basis governs, on the legal characterisation of the relationship (for example, what
  the law says the measure is where the contract is invalid, terminated or varied) and on the
  evidence of what was actually delivered or performed.
- In the group that proves the measure, name the basis you say governs, the item that evidences
  it, and in one sentence why each competing basis in the materials does not govern.

Verification: the basis, its item and the reasons against the others sit in the same group, next
to the figure.

## 2. Write the derivation where the figure is used

A derived figure appears with the values it was derived from, in the same place: the measure, the
payments proved (each with its item), any set off you accept, and the balance. Where the other side
claims a deduction you refuse, say next to the figure why it falls outside what may be deducted.
The balance in the request is the balance derived here.

## 3. Never state a figure nothing supports

Every figure and count in the filing is taken from a document in the inputs or derived in the text
from figures that are. Your own side's internal estimates, negotiating positions and fallback
figures are not evidence and never appear. If the inputs do not fix a number, argue from what they
do fix.

## 4. Counts and persons

Counts of persons, items or days behave like amounts: they need a source, and they need the
proposition they support stated next to them. Where a count in one document matches a count in an
independent document, that is a corroboration pair on the "how many" dimension; write it in the
relationship section.

## 5. Build the timeline from the documents

List, in your notes, the dated events that matter, each with the document that dates it:
formation, performance, completion or handover, acceptance or settlement, payments, demands and
replies, complaints to authorities, acknowledgements, prior proceedings, and the filing itself. A
date without a source cannot be defended.

## 6. Time bar

Where the other side raises, or its documents foreseeably support, a time bar:

1. Identify when the period started, with the event and its document.
2. List every event that interrupted it under the governing rule (a demand, an acknowledgement or
   part payment by the debtor, a complaint to a body with power to deal with it, a filing), each
   with its item and date.
3. State the consequence: the period was interrupted on each such date and ran afresh, the last
   interruption is the one that counts, and the claim was filed within the period that followed.
   Name the rule with its article number.
4. Put this in the group that carries the demands and interruptions.

Verification: the passage names the start, the interrupting events with their items, the rule, and
the conclusion, in one place. A chronology without the conclusion, or a conclusion without the
events, both fail.

Counterexample: if no event interrupted the period and the claim was filed within it, say so
directly from the start date; do not invent an interruption.

## 7. Self-check

1. Every figure, count and date traces to an input document or to a derivation written in the text.
2. The governing basis of the measure is chosen and the competing bases are answered.
3. The balance in the request equals the balance derived in the group.
4. Every time based argument states its ordering and its legal consequence.
5. Nothing the inputs leave open has been filled in with a plausible value.

## 8. Stop conditions

- Do not add a chronology, a table of figures or a calculation section that repeats the groups.
- If two documents give different values for the same quantity and nothing decides between them,
  rely on the part both support; do not pick one silently and do not ask for further material.
- Do not recompute a figure that a document states directly and no one disputes.
