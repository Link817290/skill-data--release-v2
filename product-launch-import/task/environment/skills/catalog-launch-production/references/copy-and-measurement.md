# Evidence-led storefront copy

## Draft a claim ledger before prose

For each candidate claim record:

`product → fact → number/unit → physical scope → qualification → supporting field`.

Example: `seat A → load → 140 kg → one chair → static rated load → technical specification`.
The number alone is insufficient. The same page might give a lower edge-load rating or impact limit.

## Preserve measurement meaning

- Overall width includes the frame. It does not prove usable seat width.
- Folded product dimensions do not prove packed shipping dimensions.
- Product net weight is not a carton/gross weight and does not establish a two-unit parcel weight.
- A rating per chair does not become a single combined seating capacity for a two-chair pack.
- Static, dynamic/impact, edge and distributed load ratings describe different conditions.
- “Water-resistant fabric” does not establish a waterproof complete product.

If a number is used, retain its important qualifier in nearby text. Do not hide “static” in another distant paragraph while a headline promises unrestricted capacity.

## Translation and conversion

Translate the field's role first. A terse “size” next to a diagram may mean overall open dimensions, not seat dimensions. A slash-separated height such as `36/92 cm` can mean seat/back heights; if the source does not label the components, do not assign new axis names. Keeping the original dimensional string or omitting a nonessential ambiguous dimension is safer than inventing interpretation.

Accept rounded conversions consistent with the source precision. For instance 2.4 kg is about 5.3 lb, not “exactly 5.291094 pounds.” If a source publishes incompatible conversions, do not silently choose a convenient value and pretend the discrepancy has been resolved. Omit the disputed nonessential claim or obtain clarification.

## Write useful copy, not a specification dump

Use a customer-facing title that preserves model distinctions. Introduce a practical supported use case, then provide a small set of useful material, construction, load, size or supplied-accessory facts. A benefit should follow from a real feature without becoming an untested guarantee.

“Mesh back for ventilation” can be supported by the construction. “Guaranteed sweat-free comfort in every climate” adds a performance promise. “Folding frame with a carry bag” is supported; “airline cabin approved” needs separate evidence.

## Final review branches

1. **Shared product or variant-specific fact?** If variant-specific, name the relevant option or avoid a misleading shared statement.
2. **Pack or single object?** Say what one SKU supplies; distinguish occupancy from item count.
3. **Number or implied number?** “Wide seat” may imply a seat-area claim even when the stated figure is overall width.
4. **Unknown evidence?** Absence of a load rating is not zero load and is not permission to borrow another model's rating.
5. **Nonessential conflict?** Produce good copy from undisputed facts, without making the customer page an internal discrepancy memo.


## Check a changed assumption through its dependent copy

When correcting a product or sales-unit assumption, trace every sentence that depends on it: object count, supplied parts, dimensions, load claims and packing language. Update the dependent statements together, then check independently added assertions against their own evidence. Repeating a mistaken material in a heading and a bullet is one underlying fact to repair; adding a new capacity promise requires a separate evidence check. Keep a private claim ledger so a fluent rewrite does not reintroduce a corrected assumption.
