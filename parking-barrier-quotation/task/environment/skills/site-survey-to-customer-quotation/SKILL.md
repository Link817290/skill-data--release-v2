# Site survey to customer quotation

A field record is written for the person who took it. It lists what was seen and what was agreed, in the order it came up, often with a few round figures for anything awkward to count. A customer quotation is written for two other readers: the customer, who negotiates it part by part, and the crew, who build from it. The work is the translation between those two documents, not the copying of one into the other.

## When to use

* The input is a survey record from a site visit, possibly photographed handwriting, plus photographs of the site.
* The output is a quotation the customer will sign and the crew will work from.
* House pricing rules may be given separately. Apply them as given; this skill does not supply them.

Do not use this skill to set prices. Unit prices come from the record or from the house rules you are given.

## Workflow

Keep a short working register with one row per record entry and update it as you go, so that nothing is carried forward from memory.

### 1. Read the record completely and reconcile it to itself

Transcribe every entry, including anything written between lines, in margins, or over an earlier figure. For each entry note the item, quantity, unit, figure, and whether the figure is a unit price or a line amount. Then add the entries up and compare with any total the record states. If they disagree, look for an entry you have not read before assuming the writer made an arithmetic error.

Read `references/handwritten-field-records.md` when the record is handwritten or photographed.

### 2. Understand the site before you price it

Use the photographs to understand what is being installed where: the installation points, where any central equipment sits, and roughly how far apart they are. Record what you conclude and what you had to assume.

### 3. Organise the scope the way the job will be bought and built

Arrange the lines so that a customer can see, and negotiate, the distinct parts of the job, and so that the people who build each part can find their scope.

Read `references/trade-sections.md` when deciding how to organise the lines.

### 4. Quote site works in a form that can be checked and adjusted

Round figures for work that depends on site conditions are hard for a customer to check and hard to settle once work starts.

Read `references/site-works-takeoff.md` when the record gives such work as a round figure.

### 5. Build the workbook for negotiation

Quantities will change during negotiation. Wire the workbook so that every change flows through everything that depends on it.

### 6. Describe each line for the people who will use it

Give every line a short description of what is supplied. Write only what the survey record or the house rules establish; anything the site visit did not settle belongs to the contract stage.

### 7. Reconcile before issuing

* Every register entry maps to a quotation line, or to a stated house rule that replaces it.
* Change one quantity in a copy and confirm every dependent figure moves.
* Read the document once as the customer would, without the record beside you.

## Failure modes

| What goes wrong | Why it matters |
|---|---|
| The record is copied in the order it was written | The document keeps the surveyor's view instead of the customer's and the crew's |
| An entry written between lines or over a correction is missed | Agreed scope silently disappears |
| An unclear handwritten name is guessed from the figure beside it | The customer signs for the wrong thing |
| The document is padded with terms the site visit never settled | Creates obligations nobody agreed to |

## Stop conditions

Stop and ask the person who did the survey when the register cannot be reconciled to the record's total after a second full read, or when the photographs contradict the notes.
