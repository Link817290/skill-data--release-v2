# Review Seafood Process Controls

Treat this runtime method as separate from the practitioner evidence set. It is not a
source record and cannot support a factual or compliance conclusion.

Apply this method after the general HACCP evidence boundary is set. Use the supplied
current authority for applicable values and alternatives; do not rely on remembered
limits or assume an old example remains current.

## Build the Product-Hazard Profile

Start with a product profile that preserves the distinctions on which seafood hazards
depend:

- common and scientific species identity;
- harvest or aquaculture source and receiving condition;
- whole, headed-and-gutted, fillet, minced, or other product form;
- raw, partially cooked, cooked, pasteurized, smoked, cured, dried, or fermented state;
- brine, dry salt, acid, preservative, smoke, and other formulation barriers;
- aerobic, vacuum, modified-atmosphere, or other package condition;
- refrigerated, frozen, or shelf-stable distribution;
- intended use and whether the consumer applies a kill step;
- shelf life and foreseeable exposure after the product leaves the processor.

Do not group products merely because they share a trade name or process family. Group
only when their significant hazards, CCPs, limits, and procedures are materially the
same. Record unique features explicitly.

If species or source is missing, hold open the hazards whose applicability depends on
that discriminator. Do not convert "not listed" into "not reasonably likely."

## Route Species- and Source-Dependent Hazards

Use the hazard tables as an index, then read the corresponding hazard chapter. Do not
make a significance decision from a species table alone.

For each product, check at least these routes when triggered:

- toxin formation associated with susceptible species and time-temperature history;
- parasites based on species, source, product form, and intended use;
- natural toxins based on harvest area and species;
- environmental chemicals and pesticides;
- aquaculture drugs for farm-raised product;
- allergens and species substitution;
- metal or glass introduced by equipment or handling;
- pathogenic bacteria introduced, surviving a kill step, regrowing, or contaminating
  product after the kill step.

Hazards at receipt may be controlled later only when that later control actually removes
or reduces the hazard. A cook step can address viable parasites or vegetative pathogens;
it does not reverse preformed, heat-stable chemical hazards or toxins. Preserve that
distinction.

For toxin-forming species, evaluate both harvest/receiving history and in-plant exposure.
Frozen receipt does not by itself prove that time before freezing was controlled. Route
the evidence through the receiving strategy supplied by the current authority.

## Analyze Reduced-Oxygen Packaging as a Barrier System

Reduced oxygen suppresses ordinary spoilage signals and changes which organisms can grow
before the product appears spoiled. Treat the package as a hazard trigger, not as a
neutral container.

Split the organism analysis into:

1. organisms whose spores or cells may be injured by the heating process and inhibited
   by formulation barriers;
2. more heat-resistant organisms that ordinarily rely on strict refrigeration or another
   independent barrier;
3. vegetative pathogens that may survive inadequate heating or be reintroduced after
   heating.

Map each group to a specific barrier. Do not claim that one heating step sterilizes the
product unless the supplied process authority supports that conclusion.

For smoked or cured products, distinguish:

- the brine or dry-salt recipe;
- transfer of salt or preservative into the product;
- moisture loss during smoking or drying;
- the inhibitory state of the edible portion at the end of the process;
- refrigeration during storage and distribution.

Recipe strength and residence time are process inputs. They may be useful critical
factors, but they do not by themselves prove the product reached the inhibitory state.
Require either direct lot monitoring of the product outcome or a process-establishment
study that links all critical factors to that outcome.

## Audit Brining, Salting, and Drying

When a validated scheduled process is the control strategy, identify every factor that
can change uptake or drying:

- species, cut, thickness, texture, fat, initial condition, and load;
- solution concentration, product-to-solution ratio, time, and temperature;
- dry-salt amount and uniform contact where applicable;
- chamber time, input and output temperature, humidity, airflow, loading, and smoke
  conditions where applicable;
- any preservative restrictions and maximum use levels;
- sampling position and worst-case product for confirmation.

Check that the schedule covers every product group in scope. A schedule for one species,
size, or equipment configuration cannot be silently generalized.

When the product outcome is verified by sampling, inspect sample frequency,
representativeness, analytical method, lot linkage, and action for a failing result.
Periodic verification supports an established process; it does not establish an absent
process by itself.

## Audit Heating and Smoking

First identify the control objective. It may be vegetative-pathogen destruction, spore
injury as part of a hurdle system, drying, smoke application, or more than one objective.
Each objective needs its own support.

For direct product time-temperature monitoring:

- measure the coldest location in the largest or otherwise worst-case units;
- identify how the internal sensor is placed and recorded;
- show the required duration after the target temperature is achieved;
- link the batch record to the product and scheduled process;
- check instrument range and accuracy around actual use conditions.

For process-surrogate monitoring, require a study connecting chamber conditions,
equipment settings, load, product geometry, and come-up behavior to the required product
outcome. The plan should list the monitored surrogate values, not only the result they are
intended to produce.

Do not confuse smoking chamber exposure with internal lethality. Do not assume that a
recorder measures both simply because the plan places both words in the same row.

## Audit Cooling by Product Behavior

Cooling is a dynamic process. A cold room setpoint describes the environment; it does not
show how quickly the warmest product passes through growth-supporting temperatures.

Choose monitoring based on the heat-flow direction:

- When the environment is warmer than the product, surface or ambient exposure may be
  informative.
- When the environment is colder than the product, internal temperature in a worst-case
  unit is generally the meaningful direct measure.
- A validated cooling study may support monitoring critical process factors instead of
  every product temperature, but only within the study's product, load, container,
  spacing, equipment, and starting-condition scope.

Trace cooling from the end of heating until stable refrigerated control begins. Include
delays before entry to the cooler, rack loading, packaging while warm, interruptions, and
multiple cooling stages. Do not reset cumulative exposure at a room or process boundary.

Compare the plan's cooling profile or critical factors with the applicable product
strategy in the supplied authority. If the plan only monitors room temperature, hold the
cooling-rate conclusion open unless a matching study supplies the relationship.

## Audit Refrigerated Storage and Distribution

Identify which hazard relies on refrigeration and whether refrigeration is the sole
barrier or one part of a multi-hurdle system. The target may differ across those cases.

For storage under mechanical refrigeration, check:

- continuous temperature recording where appropriate;
- placement at the warmest credible location;
- visual review of recorded data at a frequency that permits action;
- defined allowance for normal defrost behavior, if used;
- accuracy checks and calibration;
- evaluation of total exposure after an excursion.

For shipping or receipt, do not treat a package statement as evidence that temperature
was maintained. Look for carrier records, continuous exposure data, ice or cooling-media
condition, internal product temperature, transit-time limits, or another validated
strategy appropriate to the hazard.

Give credit when a plan selects a more conservative storage setting than the applicable
reference. Do not create a finding simply because its value differs from a recommended
maximum in the safer direction. Still verify that the setting is monitored and that the
rest of the barrier chain closes.

## Audit Post-Process Contamination

Separate three questions:

1. Can the pathogen survive the heating step?
2. Can it be reintroduced during exposed handling after heating?
3. If introduced, can it grow during the product's shelf life and storage conditions?

The appropriate control may be a CCP, sanitation control, prerequisite program,
environmental monitoring program, post-lethality treatment, or combination. Do not
declare a missing CCP solely because post-process contamination is conceivable.

Instead, inspect the hazard analysis and the actual supporting program. Confirm zoning,
employee and equipment traffic, exposed-product handling, cleaning and sanitizing,
environmental monitoring sites and response, and any post-lethality control. If those
records are outside the packet, report the implementation question as not determinable.

## Audit Corrective Actions for Seafood Controls

Match product evaluation to the failed control:

- formulation or drying failure requires evaluation of the affected product state and
  time-temperature history;
- heating failure requires a supported reprocess, alternate safe use, destruction, or
  other disposition based on process authority;
- cooling or refrigeration failure requires evaluation of cumulative exposure and the
  relevant organism or toxin;
- labeling failure permits relabeling only when the product itself has remained under
  control; relabeling does not reverse temperature abuse;
- instrument failure requires a lookback to the last known accurate check and an
  evaluation of affected records and lots.

"Hold for evaluation" is a containment step, not a completed disposition. Specify the
evaluation basis and the actions that regain process control.

## Reconcile the Seafood Control Chain

Before issuing a decision:

1. Reconcile product species and form to every hazard applicability decision.
2. Reconcile package atmosphere and shelf life to the barrier strategy.
3. Reconcile process inputs to the supported product outcome.
4. Reconcile each limit to what, how, where, when, and who monitors it.
5. Reconcile deviations to both product disposition and cause correction.
6. Reconcile validation evidence to the exact product/equipment scope.
7. Reconcile verification to instrument accuracy, record review, and periodic product or
   process confirmation where applicable.
8. Reconcile post-process exposure to sanitation or another documented control.
9. Mark every unresolved species, schedule, study, record, or implementation question as
   open rather than assuming the favorable state.

Revise earlier findings whenever later evidence changes the product profile or barrier
map. Finish only when every claimed control has a complete evidence path and every open
path is visible in the audit result.

## Check identity before accepting an equivalent expression

For a control endpoint, record the measured quantity, numerator and denominator,
material phase, sampling point, and process stage from the supplied authority.
Two percentages or temperatures can have the same unit while measuring different
things. For a biological target, preserve the organism or group and any subtype
or growth/toxin distinction on which the control depends. A broader hazard name
does not establish that identity. Accept an abbreviation or operational definition
only when it unambiguously denotes the same target. Derive the applicable target,
limit, and control from the supplied evidence rather than from this method.
