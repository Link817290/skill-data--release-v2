# Building the answer to a single ground

Read this once you know which grounds you must answer and what your stance is. Each move below is a way
of turning a ground into a proposition you can win, with the operation and the test that tells you the
answer is finished. Most grounds need two or three of them; almost none needs all.

A note that applies to every move: the move is not finished when the label is attached. It is finished
when the label, the facts that produce it, and the consequence for this ground are in the same passage.

## 1. Attack the element, not the story

**Trigger.** The other side's ground is a narrative that ends in a conclusion.

**Operation.** Write out what that conclusion requires. Pick the requirement that the narrative assumes
rather than argues. Answer that one, and say that the ground fails because of it.

**Verification.** Your answer names a requirement, says it is missing, and says the ground therefore
fails. If your answer is a competing narrative, you have not done this.

## 2. The rule the other side invokes has a precondition

**Trigger.** The ground rests on a rule that supplies an outcome - a default method of valuation, a
residual rule for choosing between competing instruments, a rule that fills a gap the parties left, a
tie breaker.

**Operation.** Rules of this kind are conditional by design: they apply because something is missing,
unclear, unascertainable or unagreed. Find the condition in the rule itself. Then show from the record
that the condition is not satisfied here, because the thing the rule supplies is already present. State
the condition, state what is present instead, and conclude that the rule has no application.

**Verification.** Your answer has three parts: the rule presupposes X; here X is absent because the
record contains Y; therefore the rule does not decide this. Missing any part leaves a half answer.

**Counterexample.** Where the condition really is satisfied, do not fight the rule. Fight its
application to the figures, or accept it and win the next step.

## 3. Characterisation

**Trigger.** The same facts carry different consequences depending on what they are called: whether work
is inside a scope or added to it; whether a change was a variation or a choice of method; whether an
arrangement is what its label says or what its economics say; whether an item is a separate obligation
or part of a priced whole; whether a party is acting in its own right or for someone else.

**Operation.** State the label you assert. List the features of the record that produce it - the ones the
other side's label cannot accommodate. Then state the consequence that follows from your label for this
ground, not in general.

**Verification.** Someone who disagrees with you can see exactly which features they would have to
explain away. The consequence sentence exists.

**Counterexample.** A characterisation with no consequence attached is decoration. If the label does not
change what the tribunal orders, leave it out.

## 4. Follow the consequence chain to its end

**Trigger.** You have established a proposition that changes the legal position: an instrument is
ineffective, a condition has not occurred, an obligation has not matured, a document does not bind
someone.

**Operation.** Do not stop there. Each of these has a downstream step that decides money, and the
downstream step is a separate proposition that has to be written:

- an instrument that is ineffective does not produce the payments, penalties or interest it provided
  for, and what may be recovered instead is a different and usually smaller thing;
- a condition that has not occurred means the sum is not due, which in turn means no default, which in
  turn means no penalty and no interest from the date claimed;
- an obligation that has not matured cannot be in breach, and a clause keyed to breach is not engaged;
- a document that does not bind a person cannot fix that person's liability, whatever it says.

Write each step in its own sentence, in order, under the same ground.

**Verification.** Read the chain backwards from the money. Each step is present and each follows from
the one before.

## 5. Use what the other side has already said

**Trigger.** Anywhere. This is the cheapest material in the file and the most often left unused.

**Sources.** Admissions in the opposing filing itself; recitals and preambles in the instruments the
other side relies on; the proof purpose column of its evidence list; statements the record attributes to
it; explanations it offers for why it cannot produce something; and its own conduct as recorded.

**Operation.** Quote or paraphrase it as theirs, say which of your propositions it establishes, and draw
the conclusion. Where their explanation concedes an inability to prove something, tie the concession to
the burden that rests on them.

**Verification.** The passage identifies the source as the other side's, not as a neutral fact, and says
what it establishes. "They say X" on its own does nothing.

**Counterexample.** What a decision records a party as having argued is not a finding by the tribunal,
and a report's account of what it was told is not its own conclusion. Attribute precisely; using the
wrong attribution turns a good point into a misstatement of the record.

## 6. Two documents that together fix a fact neither states

**Trigger.** A fact you need is denied, and no single document says it.

**Operation.** Find the pair. A term in one instrument plus a record of what the parties actually did;
a date in one document plus a scope in another; a confirmation of quantity expressed in units that only
one of the competing instruments uses; an identity established in one place plus an act performed in
another. State both sources and the inference in one passage, and say why the inference is the only one
the pair supports.

**Verification.** The passage names both sources. An inference with one source is an assertion; an
inference with two named sources that the other side must both explain is an argument.

## 7. Burden of proof, both limbs

**Trigger.** The other side asserts something it has not evidenced, or alleges something serious such as
fabrication, bad faith or a sham.

**Operation.** Say who must prove it and why that allocation is right, then say that they have not done
it, then say what follows for the ground. The allocation without the failure, or the failure without the
allocation, is half the move. Where the allegation is one the law expects to be proved to a high
standard, say that too.

**Verification.** Three sentences: it is theirs to prove; they have not; therefore the ground fails. The
third sentence is the one most often missing.

**Counterexample.** A burden argument is not a substitute for a positive answer where you have one. Use
it for what they assert, not for what you assert.

## 8. Distinguish things that look alike

**Trigger.** The other side relies on a document or event that is adjacent to, but not the same as, the
one that matters.

**Recurring pairs.** An inspection or acceptance of work at one stage, versus the condition of the work
later; a confirmation of one deliverable versus another; an instrument signed for an administrative
purpose versus the one the parties performed; a test or opinion obtained by one party alone versus one
obtained through the process the rules provide; a step taken in an earlier case versus the question in
this one; an estimate versus a settlement.

**Operation.** Name the difference, say why it matters for the proposition the other side draws, and say
what the document does prove, so that you are not simply denying.

**Verification.** Your sentence contains both halves: what it shows, and where it stops.

## 9. Sequencing and prior performance

**Trigger.** The other side complains that you did not do something, where its own duty came first.

**Operation.** Establish the order from the instrument - which act conditions which - then say that the
first act was not performed, then say that you were entitled to withhold the second. The entitlement is
the operative step and it is the one usually omitted; an order of performance stated without it is just
a chronology.

**Verification.** The passage ends in a right, not in a sequence.

## 10. Arguing in the alternative

**Trigger.** Your primary answer might fail, and a fallback would limit the damage.

**Operation.** Carry the alternative frame on the sentence itself and keep the fallback subordinate to
the primary. Where the fallback is a request rather than an argument, mirror it in the closing request
in the same order.

**Verification.** Each alternative sentence carries its own frame. An alternative that appears only in
the closing request, or a frame that appears only in one of several sentences making the same point,
will read as an unconditional position (see the entry's step 5).

## 11. When a ground cannot be answered on the inputs

**Trigger.** The record does not contain what you would need.

**Operation.** Say which fact is missing, say who would have to establish it, and if a procedure exists
for establishing it, name the procedure and ask for it rather than asserting the answer. Mark the point
as undetermined in your text. Do not supply the missing fact yourself; see the entry's step 4.

**Verification.** The ground is dealt with and nothing has been invented.
