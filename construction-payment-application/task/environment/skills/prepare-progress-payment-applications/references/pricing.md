# Pricing and rounding

First identify whether a rate is a contractual unit rate, an approved variation rate, a daywork rate, a provisional proposal or a supplier invoice price. They answer different questions. Apply the authority required by the contract; do not select a familiar-looking rate because it gives a plausible total.

Read the rate's inclusions. Tax-inclusive all-in rates must not receive another tax or fee layer. Conversely, a net rate requires only the adjustments actually authorized. A discount, tax rate or price-index adjustment from general practice is not a substitute for the project terms.

Keep quantity units separate from money. For example, kilograms and tonnes require a conversion before pricing; hours and shifts are not interchangeable. If a source reports cumulative volumes to three decimals, rounding quantity to a whole unit before extension changes the claim.

Use decimal arithmetic for financial values. Distinguish line rounding, component rounding and aggregate rounding. If the contract says each line is rounded to cents, sum those rounded extensions, then calculate deductions on the resulting defined base. Do not round an entire multi-line subtotal once and assume it equals the sum of rounded lines.

Show enough of the chain to reproduce an amount: item identifier, unit, current quantity, applicable price, extension and source references. Keep the source quantity and the selected price inspectable even when the completed workbook uses values instead of formulas. If formulas are used, save valid cached results or recalculate with an actual spreadsheet engine before delivery.
