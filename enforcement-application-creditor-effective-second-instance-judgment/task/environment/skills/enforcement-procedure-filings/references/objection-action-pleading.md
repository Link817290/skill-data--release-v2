# Pleading in an action arising out of an objection to enforcement

Apply this reference when a court conducting an enforcement has ruled on an objection about whether a
particular asset may be reached, and the side the ruling went against now brings an action about it.
It covers the pleading that opens that action, on either side of it.

## 1. What the action decides, and what it does not

The question is narrow and the pleading has to stay inside it: **may enforcement proceed against this
identified asset?** It is decided by asking whether the person who objected holds a civil interest in
the asset strong enough to stop enforcement against it.

What the action does not decide: whether the enforced debt exists, how much it is, whether the
underlying decision was right, or what the objector's rights are against the obligor. Those belong to
other proceedings. A pleading that argues them has changed the subject and gives the court nothing to
rule on.

Two consequences:

- **The claims are formative and declaratory, not claims for payment.** Nothing in this pleading asks
  anyone to pay anything, apart from costs.
- **The ruling is the target.** It is identified by its case number and its issuing court, in the claim
  itself and not only in the narrative.

## 2. Who sues, who is sued, and on what footing

The party the ruling went against brings the action. The other party to the objection is the defendant.

The **obligor**, that is, the party against whom the enforcement runs, is joined, and the pleading has to
decide on what footing:

- as a **co-defendant**, where its position is adverse to the claimant's, for example where it supports
  the objector's account of the transaction over the asset;
- as a **third party**, where it does not contest the claimant's position but its rights are affected.

Either footing is defensible on most records, and the choice is a professional one. What is not
optional is making it deliberately and carrying it through: the party block, the claims and the costs
claim all have to agree with the footing chosen. Where the record does not disclose the obligor's
stance, choose the footing that matches what it has done in the enforcement so far, and do not explain
the choice in the document.

Each party line carries both labels, its status in this action and its role in the enforcement case,
as `enforcement-filing-baseline.md` section 2 requires.

## 3. The claims

In this order:

1. **Set aside the ruling**, identified by its case number and its issuing court.
2. **A ruling on the asset**: that enforcement against the identified asset may proceed, or that the
   measure against it be lifted, according to which side you are on. The asset is identified as
   `enforcement-title-and-reach.md` section 3 requires, to the point where exactly one thing answers
   the description.
3. **Costs**, on the parties opposing you.

Rules on the claims:

- Do not put a money claim from the underlying case here, and do not ask for the obligor to be ordered
  to do anything about the debt.
- Do not ask the court to confirm your own substantive right as a separate claim where the second
  claim already gets you what you need. An additional declaratory claim widens the issues and invites
  a finding you did not need.
- Claim one and claim two are alternatives in form only; where the ruling is in your way, both are
  asked for, because setting the ruling aside without a direction on the asset leaves the enforcement
  where it was.
- Where local practice puts the outcome as a direction about the asset rather than as setting aside
  the ruling, either formulation is acceptable. Pick one, and do not mix them inside a single claim.

## 4. Opening the pleading: timeliness

An action of this kind must be brought within a window running from the day the ruling reached the
party. The opening sentences of the facts state the day the ruling was received, and, where it bears
on the preparation of the case, the day the other side's evidence was received, so that the court sees
the action is in time on the face of the pleading. Then state what the ruling decided, in one
sentence, and that the claimant says it is wrong.

Derive the window as part of the date chain (`enforcement-title-and-reach.md` section 4). Do not argue
timeliness; show it.

## 5. The reasoning: work the elements, do not tell the story

The protection the rules give to a person who acquired an asset before it was reached is built out of
elements. Take them one at a time and say, for each, what the record shows. The elements in the rules
that protect a buyer of immovable property typically include:

- **A valid contract of the protected kind, made before the measure was taken.** This is where most of
  these cases are decided, and it is not satisfied by the existence of a document called a contract.
  Ask what transaction the document actually performs. An arrangement that transfers the asset in
  discharge of a debt the parties already had is not the same transaction as a purchase: the
  protection is built on a buyer who paid a price for the asset, and an arrangement that extinguishes
  an existing debt has no price paid into it. Where the record shows such an arrangement, often only
  by the name of a document in an exhibit list, the element fails, and the failure is the strongest
  point in the case.
- **Possession taken before the measure**, where the rules require it.
- **The price paid**, in full or in the proportion the rules require.
- **Failure to register not attributable to the acquirer.** A finding in the ruling that registration
  was impossible for reasons on the obligor's side cuts against you here and has to be answered rather
  than ignored.
- **The purpose of the acquisition**, where the protection is confined to a person acquiring for living
  use. The nature of the asset itself often answers this: premises that are commercial by their
  registered use are not acquired for living.

**Any one element failing is enough.** Say so expressly, then argue the elements that fail on this
record and do not labour the rest. A pleading that argues all of them equally suggests none of them is
strong.

**Read the exhibit list against itself, not through it.** The other side's stated purpose for each
exhibit is its argument, not a finding. The decisive fact in this family is often the bare name of a
document in a list, whose legal character nobody has characterised. Go through the names.

## 6. Ranking, where both sides hold an interest

Where the objector does hold some interest, the question becomes which interest prevails, and that is
a comparison, not an assertion. State what your client's claim is and what gives it its rank, since a
statutory priority the concluded decision has already recognised ranks above an ordinary money claim
and above an unregistered contractual interest; which interest the other side actually holds and where
that one sits; and why the interests the rules shelter are not engaged on these facts. Where the
protection exists to shelter a home, saying that the asset is not a home is the comparison, not a side
point.

## 7. Authority

Element questions in this family are settled by published authority more often than by the text of a
statute.

- Cite the rules on objections to enforcement and on their review for the elements they fix, naming the
  instrument and the article, and do not invent the number.
- Where a point is settled by guidance the highest court has published, such as a conference
  summary, a reply or a designated case, name the issuing body, the series and the year, state the proposition it
  settles, and keep the quotation to what carries the proposition. A long block quotation of the whole
  item pads the pleading and buries the point.
- Do not extend an authority past its own situation. Guidance on one kind of arrangement decides that
  kind of arrangement.
- Where you cannot place an authority you remember, state the proposition and the instrument without a
  number rather than guessing one.

## 8. The boundary on suspicion

Records in these cases are thin, and the temptations are specific: that a contract was back-dated, that
it was never filed with the registry, that the transaction was collusive. Where the record neither
establishes nor excludes such a point, it may be put once in the form of something the record does not
exclude, and it may not be asserted, repeated, or relied on by a later paragraph or by a claim. See
`unsupported-specifics-and-placeholders.md` section 4. Where the record does establish an absence,
a registry extract showing no filing for instance, that is a fact and is stated as one.

## 9. Closing

One sentence that asks the court to grant the claims, naming the outcome on the asset. It adds no new
ground and introduces no new figure.

## 10. Stop conditions

- **A claim for money from the underlying case** does not go in this pleading, however plainly it is
  owed.
- **An element you cannot support on the record** is not argued as though you could; drop it and rely
  on the ones you can.
- **A concession**, that registration was the acquirer's right, that the price was paid, or that the
  action may be out of time, never appears, even as a hedge.
- **Where your client's position depends on a fact only the obligor can supply**, plead what the record
  shows and stop; do not ask, inside the pleading, for it to be produced.

## 11. Self-check

1. The pleading decides one question only: whether enforcement may reach the identified asset.
2. Every party line carries both labels, and the obligor's footing is stated and consistent throughout.
3. The claims set aside the identified ruling by case number, direct the outcome on the asset, and ask
   for costs; no payment claim appears.
4. The asset description admits of exactly one thing.
5. The opening states the day the ruling was received and shows the action is in time without arguing
   it.
6. Every element relied on is stated as an element, with what the record shows on it, and the pleading
   says that one failure suffices.
7. Every authority is named with its issuing body and, for a statute or interpretation, its article;
   nothing is quoted beyond what carries the proposition; no number is invented.
8. Nothing the record neither establishes nor excludes is asserted, and anything put in weakened form
   appears once and carries no weight.
9. Nothing concedes a point against your client anywhere, including in the closing.
