# Variations, site visas, unit price adjustment, delay, employer failures, and price changes

Apply this when the dispute involves work added or removed, site visas or instructions, a reduced
quantity, delay or its attribution, conditions the employer undertook to supply and did not, or movements in
material or labour prices. Each section gives the trigger, the operation, the check, and when not to use
it. Cite the current provision for each rule with its article number beside its proposition.

## 1. Added work and changes of scope

**Trigger.** Work added by design changes or site instructions; a lump sum or fixed price contract.

**Operation.**
- A lump sum covers the scope defined when it was agreed. Work added by a later design change or
  instruction sits outside it and is separately payable. Frame this as a separate request for the added
  work, identified by what was added (the element, the drawing revision, or the instruction), not as a
  general complaint of loss.
- Where the claimed settlement figure (a submitted final account, an agreed account) already includes
  the variations, visas, and price adjustments, they are components of that figure. Argue them in the
  facts as parts of the price; do not add them as separate requests, which would count them twice.

## 2. Valuing changed work: state the layer you rely on

The rule on valuing changed work has layers; name the layer and cite its article:
1. The parties' agreed pricing standard or method governs.
2. Where a design change altered the quantity or the quality standard and the parties cannot agree the
   price of that part, the pricing method or standard published by the local construction authority at
   the time of contracting may be referred to.
3. The quantity actually done is proved by visas and other written records made during construction,
   and failing those by other evidence. Visas prove quantity; they are not the valuation rule.

The valuation route and its rule sit in the facts; the request names the object, the amount or a bare
marker, and nothing else.

## 3. Site visas and instructions

**Trigger.** The claim relies on visas, instructions, or quantity confirmations, and the employer has
disowned some of them.

**Operation.**
1. In your notes, list the visas relied on and the ones the employer disputes, with the ground of each
   dispute (the signatory lacked authority, the signatory had left, no seal, the work was within the
   lump sum) and the document where the employer raised it.
2. In the complaint, identify the disputed set: how many, which ones (by date range, subject, or
   number if short), and on what ground the employer disputes them. Then say why the signature binds:
   a visa signed by the employer's site representative within that role binds the employer, including
   after the person leaves; point to what shows the role (the contract's appointment clause, the
   employer's earlier acceptance of that person's visas, payments made against them), and cite the rule
   on acts of an agent within the authority the principal held out.
3. Say who signed a visa only when the record shows the signatory. A visa list that does not name the
   signer does not support a sentence that the employer signed it.

**Check.** The complaint names the disputed visas and the employer's ground before answering it, and
every statement about a signatory has a document.

## 4. A quantity reduced by the employer: unit price adjustment

**Trigger.** The employer reduced the quantity of an item sharply, and the contractor's rate for that
item carried fixed costs or margin spread over the original quantity.

**Operation.**
- The remedy the valuation rules give is an adjustment of the unit price for the remaining quantity once
  the deviation passes the threshold set by the national bill of quantities valuation code. The request
  names the item, states the direction (upward for a reduced item whose rate carried fixed costs),
  applies it to the remaining quantity, and claims the difference, with the amount or a bare marker.
- The code, its edition, its clause number, and the threshold go in the facts, with why the reduction
  strands costs spread over the original quantity.
- A client's grievance that "the profit was in the part they cut" is this remedy in law; do not claim it
  as general lost profit.

**Check.** The request says upward (or downward) in words and names the item and the remaining
quantity; the code and threshold are only in the facts.

## 5. Delay: attribution before aggregation

**Trigger.** The employer claims delay damages, or the contractor claims time and cost for delay, or the
contract term needs to be placed against the actual dates.

**Operation.**
1. **Planned start.** Fix it from the contract term (for example, a stated number of days after
   signing), not from a ceremony, a site entry, or a start notice. Fix the actual start from the start
   notice or the first site record. Compute the difference in days and state both dates and the count.
2. **Contractual completion.** Run the contract duration from the actual start, not from signing,
   unless the contract says otherwise, and state the resulting completion date. Compare it with the
   actual completion or the date the employer required. If the employer required an earlier completion
   and the contractor achieved it, say so: that is acceleration at the employer's request.
3. **Attribution, in notes only.** List each delaying event with its cause, its days, and its document.
   Mark each as employer-caused, neutral (force majeure, public measures), or contractor-caused. Do not
   call a neutral event employer-caused.
4. **Aggregation.** Only then compare: the delay attributable to the employer and neutral events
   against the overrun. An aggregate that "covers" the overrun proves nothing unless each component is
   attributed and the periods do not overlap.
5. **Clause search.** Search the full contract for the clause that addresses each event (a start notice
   issued late, a suspension ordered by the employer, a price adjustment after a long
   suspension or delayed start). A summary that cites one clause may have missed the one that governs.
   Rely on the clauses that support the plaintiff; a clause that would cut down a request is for the
   defendant to invoke, and the complaint neither cites it nor reduces a request on its account.
6. **Writing it.** The complaint states the planned start, the actual start and the difference, the
   contractual completion, any acceleration, and the attributed result in a sentence or two. It does not
   reproduce the list, neither as a table nor as a run of dates and visa numbers inside one sentence.

**Check.** Every delay day relied on appears once in your notes with its cause and source; the complaint
states the dates that carry the conclusion and no more.

## 6. Employer failures and the contractor's claim for time and cost

**Trigger.** The employer did not provide the site, the drawings, the conditions, the equipment, or the
coordination it undertook to provide, and the contractor's method, sequence, or resources had to change,
or stood idle, as a result.

**Operation.** For each failure, state the undertaking, the failure, the consequence on the works, and
the cost it caused, and cite the rule that lets the contractor claim time and cost when the employer
fails to provide the agreed conditions. Request compensation for the resulting costs when the inputs give
their amount or base, or with a bare marker. The time lost is argued in the facts and reasons, where it
answers any delay objection; it is not made a separate request to confirm an extension of time. Make it a
request only when the client's instructions ask for it.

**Not applicable** to costs the contract clearly allocates to the contractor's risk. Where that
allocation is arguable, claim the costs and leave the argument to the defendant.

## 7. Material and labour price changes

**Trigger.** Prices of main materials or labour moved during performance, or a long suspension or late
start pushed the works into a later price period.

**Operation.** Rely first on the contract's own adjustment clause (after a long suspension, on a
delayed start, on published labour rate changes); find it in the full contract. Where there is none, or
the employer refused to apply it, state the movement with its source and period, show that it exceeds
what a party could foresee at formation, show the request for renegotiation and the refusal, and request
the adjustment under the change of circumstances rule, stating its direction and amount or marker. When
the claimed settlement figure already includes the adjustment, it is argued as a component, not
requested again.

## 8. Self-check

1. Each change request names what changed, the amount or marker, and the direction; the valuation layer
   and its rule are in the facts; nothing already inside the claimed price is requested again.
2. The disputed visas are identified with the employer's ground before the answer; no signatory is
   asserted without a document.
3. Planned start, actual start, difference, and contractual completion are stated with their dates;
   delay conclusions rest on attributed, non-overlapping days.
4. The clause relied on for each event is the one in the contract that governs it; no clause adverse to
   the plaintiff is cited to reduce a request.
5. No request confirms an extension of time or any other matter that only supports a reason.
