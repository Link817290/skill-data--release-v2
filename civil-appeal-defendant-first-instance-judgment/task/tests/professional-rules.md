# Attacking a decision that has already been made

Every document in this family starts from the same place: a tribunal has ruled, its ruling is
written down, and you are asking a different or higher body to undo part of it. The reader already
has the decision. They do not need it retold. They need to be shown, for each part of it you attack,
what the tribunal held, why that holding cannot stand on the record it had, and what should replace
it.

## What these deliverables are, and what they are not

A challenge to a decision is organised around the decision, not around the dispute. The dispute was
already tried. What is live now is a finite list of operative determinations, and your document has
to say which of them you want changed and into what.

Three consequences follow. They are the ones most often ignored, and each of them costs more than it
looks.

- **The operative items set the agenda, not the grievances.** A reviewing body can revoke, vary, or
  send back an operative determination. It cannot grade the decision below. An objection that does
  not resolve into a change to a listed determination has nowhere to land.
- **You cannot ask for more than the posture allows.** Relief your side never claimed below, and
  relief that no attacked determination disposed of, is not available here merely because it would
  be welcome. Reserving the right to pursue something separately is not the same as asking for it.
- **Who the document is addressed to decides what belongs in it.** A filing goes to the tribunal and
  contains only what you are asking the tribunal to do and why. An assessment goes to the client and
  is expected to contain the things a filing must never contain: time limits, prospects, the weak
  points of your own position, what to do next. Settle the addressee before drafting, because the
  same sentence is required in one and disqualifying in the other. See
  [references/filing-types.md](references/filing-types.md).

## Step 1: rebuild the decision as a list of operative items

Before reading anything for grounds, take the decision apart. One row per operative determination,
in the order the decision lists them. For each row record what was ordered, which of the parties'
claims it disposes of, and the chain that produced it: the evidence the tribunal said it relied on,
the facts it found, the rule it applied, and the arithmetic that produced any figure.

Then mark each row: favourable, adverse, or neutral to your side. The adverse rows are the only
candidates for attack, and the favourable rows are a constraint, because asking to undo a
determination you benefit from is a drafting accident that is easy to make and expensive to make.

[references/judgment-anatomy.md](references/judgment-anatomy.md) sets out how to do this when the
decision is long, when its reasoning and its operative part disagree, and when an earlier decision
sits behind the one you are attacking.

Verification: every operative item in the decision appears in exactly one row, and every row carries
a stance. A decision with items you did not index is a decision you have not read.

## Step 2: decide, item by item, what you are asking for

For each adverse row, choose one outcome and write it down before you look for reasons:

- **revoke** the determination and have it replaced with a stated one, where you can state what the
  correct determination is;
- **revoke** and have the matter reheard, where the correct determination cannot be reached on the
  present record;
- **vary** the determination, where the shape is right and a figure, a period, a rate, or a scope is
  wrong.

Where the outcome is a figure, it is a specific figure, and you must be able to produce it from the
record. Where it is a period or a rate, say from when and on what base.

Keep this in step with step 3, and keep it anchored. The figures the decision used and the figures
the record establishes are the ones the reviewing body can work with, so state your outcome as those
figures adjusted by the components you actually attack: the award as found, plus or minus this
ground and that one, shown as a short chain a reader can follow back to the decision.

Do not rebuild the whole account from your own reading of the record. An independently recomputed
total may be the better number and still be unusable, because the reviewing body cannot trace it to
anything it has already found, and every request, declaration and interest base computed from it
inherits the same problem. If your work shows that a figure the decision treated as settled is wrong,
that is a ground of its own: attack it, quantify the correction, and carry the correction into the
chain. What you must not do is quietly adopt a different starting figure and present the result as
arithmetic.

The same discipline runs the other way. A total that still carries a component you decided not to
argue is a figure your own grounds no longer support, so drop the component and say that you have.

Counterexample: do not write the outcome as an aspiration. An outcome stated as a direction, for
example that a sum should be increased, gives the reviewing body nothing to enter.

## Step 3: select the grounds, which means rejecting most of them

Apply one threshold to every candidate error you have found:

> If this ground succeeded, and every other ground failed, would any operative item change?

The threshold is a test of effect, not of size. A ground that moves a figure in an operative item
passes, and it passes whether the figure is large or small: the reviewing body can grant it, and a
sum you do not ask for is a sum you do not get. Do not drop a ground because the amount looks minor
next to the others, and do not drop it because it is the eighth. If the decision refused it and you
want it back, it is a ground.

What fails the threshold is the objection that changes nothing whatever happens to it: a criticism of
the reasoning that leaves every operative item where it was, a procedural complaint that would have
produced the same result, a point already fully carried by another ground. Those have two
dispositions: fold them into a ground that does pass, as support, or drop them.

Keeping a true dead end costs you. It dilutes the grounds that matter and invites the reviewing body
to answer your document at its weakest point. But dropping a live one costs more, because it is gone
for good.

Where the challenge is one that may only be founded on grounds the statute enumerates, the
enumeration is a second filter applied after this one, and it is a filter, not an outline. Run your
surviving grounds through it and discard what does not fit. Do not organise the document by the
enumerated categories, do not open a section for a category you have nothing to put in, and do not
add a passage asserting that the grounds reinforce one another: a reviewing body that is not
persuaded by any single ground is not persuaded by their sum.

[references/ground-selection.md](references/ground-selection.md) covers how to test a candidate
against the threshold when its effect is indirect, and how to handle a cause that produces errors
across several items.

## Step 4: build each surviving ground

One ground, one section. The section has to establish four things:

- what the decision held, quoted or closely paraphrased, located so the reader can find it;
- why it cannot stand, worked against the record the tribunal had;
- what the correct determination is;
- which operative item this changes, and by how much.

Write them as continuous legal prose, in the order the argument needs. They are what the section must
contain, not a form to fill in. Do not give them labelled sub-blocks, and do not separate the finding
you attack from the reason it fails: a reader who has to hold three headings in mind to see one
argument is reading a worksheet. Keep each proposition, with the reason that supports it, inside the
paragraph that makes it.

The second is the whole of the work. A section that establishes the other three and asserts this one
is a complaint. [references/ground-selection.md](references/ground-selection.md) sets out the forms
this reasoning takes and how to layer alternatives without contradicting yourself.

When your ground turns on a figure, a period, a rate, or a quantity, the recomputation belongs here
and belongs visibly: see [references/figures-and-arithmetic.md](references/figures-and-arithmetic.md).

## Step 5: close every ground into the relief, and close the relief into the items

Two directions, both mandatory, and a document that passes one but not the other is unfinished.

Forward: each ground ends by naming the relief it supports. A ground whose effect never reaches a
request has not been finished.

Backward: each request names the operative item it addresses and what should happen to it. A request
supported by no ground is either an oversight in the grounds or an overreach in the requests, and you
have to decide which before filing.

The figures in the requests are the figures the grounds produced. Where two grounds affect the same
item, the request carries the combined figure and the grounds each show their own contribution.

[references/relief-and-closure.md](references/relief-and-closure.md) covers ordering, costs, interest
and ancillary relief, and what to do when the relief you want is available only in part.

## Proportion

Length follows the argument, not the record. The requests state the relief and nothing else. The
grounds carry the reasoning, and within a ground the space goes to why the holding cannot stand, not
to reproducing what the decision, the contract or the expert report already say. Quote what you
attack, quote what contradicts it, and stop.

A document that runs well past the length the same grounds would take in the decision itself is
usually one that restated the record instead of arguing with it.

## Step 6: stop where the document stops

A filing ends with the relief and the signature block. There is no closing summary, no recapitulation
of the grounds, no statement of confidence in the tribunal, and no appended note to your own client.
If you have written something that would have to be deleted before filing, it did not belong in the
document; it belonged in your notes or in a covering communication.

An assessment addressed to the client ends where its own type says it ends, which is a different
place. Check the type before you apply this step.

## Self-check before saving

Run this against your notes, not against your memory of drafting.

1. Every operative item of the decision is indexed and carries a stance.
2. Every ground in the document passes the threshold in step 3, and you can say which item it moves.
3. Every request names an operative item and a disposition; no request asks for relief the posture
   does not allow; no favourable item is caught by a request to revoke.
4. Every ground closes into a request; every request is supported by a ground; the figures agree in
   both directions.
5. Every figure you assert is quoted from the record or derived, and every derivation is shown.
6. Nothing is present that the addressee of this document should not receive.
7. The parties, their designations in this proceeding, the body addressed, and the decision under
   attack are correct and consistent throughout.
8. The deliverable is saved in the requested format, name, and location.

## Failure modes

These are the ways this family of document goes wrong. Each one is a failure of selection or of
addressee, not of effort.

| Failure | What it looks like | Where it is caught |
|---|---|---|
| Breadth mistaken for strength | Many grounds, several turning on sums too small to move an item | Step 3 threshold |
| Grievance with no landing | A section attacking the decision's reasoning that resolves into no change | Step 1 index, step 5 backward pass |
| Relief invented at the end | A request for something never claimed below, or disposed of by no attacked item | Step 2, self-check 3 |
| Addressee confusion | Time limits, prospects, risk warnings, or instructions to the client inside a filing | Step 6, filing-types reference |
| Categories used as an outline | Sections named after enumerated statutory grounds, one of them empty or padded | Step 3 |
| Blanks where derivation was possible | A placeholder standing in for a figure the record supports | Step 4, figures reference |
| Silent loss of a request | An operative item you meant to have varied, attacked in the grounds but absent from the relief | Step 5 forward pass |


# Figures, periods and derivation

Read this whenever a ground turns on an amount, a quantity, a rate, or a period, which in this family
of document is most of the time.

## Derive rather than leave blank

The default is that a figure you need is obtainable. The record contains the components, and the work
is to find them, join them, and run the arithmetic. A document that reports a figure as unavailable
when the record supports it has lost the point the figure was carrying.

A placeholder is correct in one situation only: the record genuinely does not establish the value and
no derivation reaches it. Then mark it visibly as missing and say what is missing. It is not correct
as a way of avoiding the arithmetic, and it is not correct for a value that follows from figures the
record does contain.

Do not supply a value the record does not support, including inside brackets and including when
labelled as an estimate. A supplied number is a supplied number whatever it is wrapped in.

## Show the derivation where the figure is new

Any figure that does not appear in the record in that form is new, and a new figure is stated with
the arithmetic that produces it and the source of each component. Written out, so a reader can check
it without rebuilding your working.

This matters twice over. A reviewing body cannot enter a figure it cannot verify. And a figure
presented without its derivation is indistinguishable, to a reader, from one that was estimated.

Where a component is itself derived, show that derivation too, or carry it in a short table of
components with their sources. Depth of two is usually enough; beyond that, table it.

## Reconcile against what the decision did

For every figure you assert, place it against the figure the decision used, and account for the whole
of the difference. An unexplained residue means one of them is wrong and you do not yet know which.

Where the decision reached its figure by a method you say is wrong, run both methods and show both
results. Where the decision's arithmetic is internally wrong, run the decision's own method correctly
and show the result, because that is a narrower and more easily granted ground than replacing the
method.

## Double counting

Deductions and offsets are where double counting hides. Where a sum has been removed once by one
route, check every other route by which the same sum could have been removed again. This is found by
listing every deduction the decision applied, with what each was for, and looking for two entries
that answer to the same underlying item under different names.

The same discipline applies to your own document. A component you add under two grounds is a
component you have claimed twice.

## Periods, rates and bases

A monetary claim that runs over time has three parts, and all three have to be stated and justified
separately: from when, at what rate, on what base. A ground that fixes one of them leaves the other
two as the decision had them, and the request has to say so.

The start date is the part most often asserted rather than established. Name the event that starts it
and where the record puts that event.

Where a base changes because a ground succeeded, the period and rate calculation has to be rerun on
the new base, and the request carries the rerun figure.

## Quantities and units

Where the dispute is over quantity, the unit is part of the claim. Confirm that the quantity the
decision used and the quantity you assert are expressed in the same unit and measured on the same
basis before comparing them. A quantity comparison across two bases is not a comparison.

## Verification

- Every figure in the relief appears in a ground, with its derivation.
- Every derivation's components are sourced to the record.
- The difference between your figure and the decision's figure is fully accounted for.
- No component reaches the total by two routes.
- Every time based sum states its start, rate and base.
- Every placeholder marks a value the record genuinely does not establish.


# Which document you are writing

Read the routing test first. Then read only the section it sends you to. The sections state rules
that are correct for their own type and wrong for the others, so applying one type's rule to another
type's deliverable produces a confident, well written, disqualifying document.

## Routing test

Two questions, in this order.

**Who receives it?** If the deliverable is addressed to the tribunal, it is a filing. If it is
addressed to your own side, it is an assessment. The assignment usually says so. Where it does not,
the deliverable's name and the thing it is expected to contain will: a document expected to reach a
recommendation about whether to proceed is an assessment, whatever it is called.

**Has the decision taken effect?** If the decision is still open to ordinary challenge, the filing is
an ordinary challenge and the reviewing body's scope is set by what you raise. If it has taken
effect, the filing is an extraordinary challenge and the grounds are limited to those the law
enumerates.

Three destinations follow.

| Receives it | Decision status | Section |
|---|---|---|
| The tribunal | Still open to ordinary challenge | A |
| The tribunal | Has taken effect | B |
| Your own side | Either | C |

If the routing is genuinely ambiguous after both questions, say so in one line at the top of your
notes, choose the reading the assignment's deliverable name supports, and draft that one. Do not
hedge by writing a document that serves both; there is no such document.

## Section A: ordinary challenge to a decision not yet in effect

The reviewing body's scope is set by what you put in your requests. This has two consequences that
run in opposite directions, and both are live.

You are not confined to the grounds the other side is willing to discuss, and you are not confined to
the categories the decision used. Anything in the decision that is adverse to you and that you ask to
have changed is in scope.

You are confined by your own requests. A determination you do not ask to have changed will not be
changed, including one that becomes obviously wrong once another of your grounds succeeds. Work the
consequences through: when a ground moves a figure, find every other determination that was computed
from that figure and bring each into the requests.

Your position below constrains you. Relief your side did not claim below is not available by raising
it now. Where the decision refused part of a claim you did make, that refusal is attackable even
though it appears only as the gap between what was claimed and what was granted.

Where both sides are dissatisfied, your document addresses your own grievances. It is not the place
to answer what the other side may raise; that is a separate document, written later, against what
they actually raised.

Structure: the parties and their designations in this proceeding, the decision attacked, the
requests, the grounds, the signature block. It ends there.

## Section B: extraordinary challenge to a decision that has taken effect

The decision is final and the presumption is against reopening it. Two things follow that do not
apply in section A.

**The grounds are a closed list.** The law enumerates the circumstances in which a decision that has
taken effect may be reopened. An error that is real, demonstrable, and outside the list is not a
ground here. Apply the list after your own materiality selection, as a filter: take the grounds that
survived the threshold and ask, for each, which enumerated circumstance it falls within. Discard what
falls within none.

This is a filter, not an outline. Three specific errors follow from treating it as an outline, and
all three are common:

- Opening a section for each enumerated circumstance, including ones you have nothing to put in.
- Naming sections after the circumstances rather than after what was wrongly decided, which buries
  the substance and makes the document hard to answer in your favour.
- Adding a passage saying that the grounds are mutually reinforcing or together show a pattern. They
  are assessed individually. A reader unpersuaded by each one separately is not persuaded by the
  observation that there are several.

**The relief has an extra first step.** You are asking the body to reopen the case, and then to
change the determinations. Ask for both, in that order, and name every decision that has to be undone
for the relief to be worth anything, including any earlier decision that still stands beneath the one
you attack.

Where the challenge rests on material not before the tribunal below, that material has to satisfy the
conditions the law attaches to it. Establish those conditions expressly; asserting that the material
is new and important does not establish them.

Cite the enumerated grounds consistently and in the form currently in force. Using two different
numbering schemes in one document, which happens when sources of different vintages are combined,
tells the reader the citations were not checked.

Structure: the parties and their designations across the proceedings including this one, the
decisions attacked, the requests beginning with the request to reopen, the grounds, the signature
block. It ends there.

## Section C: assessment addressed to your own side

This is not a filing and the reticence a filing requires is wrong here. Its purpose is to let someone
decide whether to proceed, and a decision cannot be made from a document that only argues one way.

It contains what the filings must not:

- The procedural clock. When the period runs from, when it expires, what starts it, and what is not
  yet known that is needed in order to fix it.
- The weaknesses. Which of your grounds depends on a fact not yet established, which was already
  rejected below and why, and where the prospects are poor. A ground you would run but would not bet
  on is worth saying so about.
- The recommendation. Which determinations to challenge, which to leave, and why, including the ones
  you recommend leaving.
- What has to be obtained before filing.

It also contains the case posture in a way a filing does not: who the parties are, what was claimed,
what was granted and refused, and how the decision's operative items map onto the claims. The reader
may not have the decision in front of them, and unlike the tribunal, they were not there.

Where the assignment asks the assessment to carry a draft of the filing, the draft is a part of this
document and is written to section A or section B as applicable. Keep it separate and visibly so.
The assessment's frankness stops at the boundary of the draft.

Structure: follow what the assignment asks for. Where it does not specify, posture and history, then
the analysis by operative item, then the recommendation, then the procedural requirements, then any
draft.


# Choosing grounds and building them

Read this at step 3 and again at step 4.

## Applying the threshold when the effect is indirect

The threshold is whether this ground, succeeding alone, changes an operative item. Most candidates
answer it immediately. Three kinds do not.

**The ground that changes an input to a figure.** Trace it through. If the item's figure was produced
by an arithmetic chain, a ground that corrects one input passes only if you can carry the correction
through the chain and land on a different figure. Do the carry before you decide to keep it. Often
the correction is absorbed: the tribunal reached the same number by a route your correction does not
touch, and the ground fails the threshold even though it is right.

**The ground that changes who bears a burden.** A misallocated burden of proof passes the threshold
only where the party who should have borne it did not discharge it. Where both sides put in evidence
and the tribunal weighed it, the allocation made no difference to the outcome and the ground is
support for a fact ground, not a ground.

**The procedural ground.** Ask what the correct procedure would have produced. A procedural defect
that would have produced the same determination does not move an item, however irregular it was. A
procedural defect that prevented a fact from being established moves the item that rests on that
fact, and is best presented that way, joined to the fact rather than free standing.

## One cause, many instances

Sometimes a run of errors shares one cause. Then you have a choice, and both answers are wrong in
different circumstances, so the distinction matters more than it looks.

**Merge only within one determination, and only when the instances are the same error.** A tribunal
that applied one wrong rate to eleven line items of a single award made one error eleven times. State
the cause once, list the instances with their amounts, total them, and let the total reach the relief.
Presented this way a set of individually immaterial errors can pass the threshold together, which is
the only circumstance in which small items belong in the document at all.

**Never merge across determinations, claim heads, or cost categories.** Two refusals that happen to
share a legal theory are still two refusals. Each was decided separately, each has its own evidence,
its own amount, and its own place in the operative part, and the reviewing body has to be able to
grant one without the other. Folding them into a single ground because the argument is similar costs
you the one you did not name: it disappears from the document, and with it the amount it carried.

The test: fix the cause and ask whether every instance is fixed, and ask whether a reviewing body
could sensibly accept your ground as to one instance and reject it as to another. If it could, they
are separate grounds however similar the reasoning, and each gets its own section.

Counterexample: a refusal of haulage costs and a refusal of a price adjustment may both rest on the
same clause of the tender documents. They are still two grounds. Argue the clause once if you like,
but carry each refusal, each amount, and each request separately.

## The forms the reasoning takes

Step 4 point 2 is where the document is won. There are a limited number of forms it takes, and
naming the one you are using keeps the section honest.

- **The record contradicts the finding.** Strongest, and available more often than expected. Requires
  that you produce the passage of the record and show it cannot be reconciled with what was found.
  Not available where the record merely also supports your version; that is a weight argument.
- **The finding rests on nothing.** The tribunal states a conclusion and names no evidence, or names
  evidence that does not address the proposition. Show what the finding needed and what was offered.
- **The finding rests on material that should not have been relied on.** Attack the admissibility or
  the reliability of the material, then show the finding does not survive without it. Both halves are
  required; the second is the one that gets dropped.
- **The rule does not fit.** Set out the rule's elements and walk them against the found facts.
  Identify the element that fails. A rule argument that does not name the failing element is an
  assertion of disagreement.
- **The tribunal applied its own holding inconsistently.** It held a clause inapplicable and then
  applied it, or held a method unsound and then used its result.
- **The arithmetic is wrong.** A component omitted, counted twice, priced on the wrong basis, or a
  period or base misstated. See the figures reference.

## Layering alternatives without contradicting yourself

Where you have a primary and a fallback, order them so the fallback is expressly conditional, and
make the condition the failure of the primary rather than a fact. Written as alternative facts, the
two positions read as uncertainty about your own case and each weakens the other. Written as
alternative consequences of the same facts, they do not.

The fallback must still land somewhere. A fallback that produces no determinable outcome is not a
fallback.

## Stopping conditions

- A candidate that fails the threshold is folded in as support, or dropped. It does not become a
  short section.
- A ground that requires a fact not in the record is not a ground in this document. Where a route
  exists for putting new material before the reviewing body, that is a separate application with its
  own requirements; it is not cured by asserting the fact in the grounds.
- A ground that would, if accepted, undo a determination favourable to you is abandoned or confined.


# Taking the decision apart

Read this at step 1, before you look for anything to attack. The index you build here is what every
later step is checked against.

## The unit of work is the operative determination

A decision has a narrative part, a reasoning part, and an operative part. Only the operative part
binds. Index that part first, one row per determination, in the decision's own order and using its
own numbering, because every later reference you make to it has to be findable by a reader holding
the decision.

For each row, record five things:

| Field | What goes in it |
|---|---|
| Ordered | What the party must do, or what is declared, or what is refused |
| Disposes of | Which pleaded claim, in whole or in part, this answers |
| Chain | The evidence named, the facts found, the rule applied, the arithmetic run |
| Stance | Favourable, adverse, or neutral to your side |
| Delta | Where adverse and quantified, the gap between what was claimed and what was ordered |

The Delta column is where the document's substance usually is. A determination that grants part of a
claim is two facts: what was granted, and what was refused without being listed as a refusal. The
refused part is frequently the largest thing in the case and the easiest to leave out of the index,
because it does not appear as an operative item of its own.

## When the reasoning and the operative part disagree

This happens more often than it should, and when it happens it is one of the strongest available
grounds, because it needs no evidence beyond the decision itself.

Two forms to look for:

- The reasoning accepts a proposition and the operative part proceeds as though it had been rejected,
  or the reverse.
- Two passages of reasoning cannot both be true, and each supports a different determination.

Record these in the index as you find them, against the row they affect. Do not start drafting them
yet; they still have to pass the step 3 threshold, and an internal contradiction that changes nothing
is still not a ground.

## A claim the decision never reached

Compare the index against the pleaded claims. A claim that appears in no row was not decided. That
is a different defect from a claim decided wrongly, it usually has a different remedy, and it is
found only by running the comparison deliberately. The decision will not flag it.

## When there is a decision behind the decision

Where what you attack is itself a review of an earlier decision, index both, and align them row by
row. Three cases, and they are not interchangeable:

- The later decision adopted the earlier determination. Your ground has to reach the reasoning that
  adopted it, not only the original reasoning, because the adoption is what now stands.
- The later decision replaced it. The earlier reasoning is history and attacking it wastes the
  reader's attention.
- The later decision is silent on it while changing something it depended on. This is a live gap and
  it is worth indexing separately.

Where the relief you seek requires both decisions to be undone, both have to be named in the relief.
A request that removes only the later one leaves the earlier one standing and gives you nothing.

## Verification before you leave this step

- Every operative item is in the index, including refusals expressed as a partial grant.
- Every pleaded claim maps to an item, or is recorded as not reached.
- Every adverse row has a Delta, or a note saying why it is not quantifiable.
- Nothing in the index is a grievance yet. This step produces a map, not an argument.


# The relief, and closing the document into it

Read this at step 5.

## What a request has to contain

Each request names the operative item it addresses, using the decision's own numbering, and states
what should happen to it. Three shapes, and the choice is substantive:

- Revoke and substitute, where you can state the substituted determination in full, including any
  figure.
- Revoke and remit, where the correct determination cannot be reached on the present record. Be able
  to say what has to be established on the rehearing; a remission requested because the substituted
  determination is hard to formulate will be read, correctly, as an admission that you cannot
  formulate it.
- Vary, where the determination's shape is right and one element of it is wrong.

A request that asks for a change without naming the item leaves the reviewing body to guess which
part of its own decision you mean.

## Ordering

Follow the decision's order of operative items, not the order of your grounds and not the order of
importance. The reader is holding the decision. Costs come last, after the substantive requests,
because their allocation depends on the outcome of everything above them.

## Costs, fees and expenses

Costs of the proceedings below and of this proceeding are dealt with together and expressly. Where
the case involved an expert assessment, an appraisal, a preservation measure, or any other separately
incurred expense, each is named. A general request that costs be borne by the other side is not a
request about a fee it does not mention.

Where your substantive requests succeed only in part, the costs request has to be capable of applying
proportionately.

## Ancillary and declaratory relief

Relief that depends on a substantive determination, for example a declared priority or a security
interest expressed as a proportion of a sum, moves when the sum moves. State it as a function of the
sum you are asking for, not as a fixed figure copied from the pleading below, and check it after the
substantive figures are final.

Do not introduce ancillary relief here that no attacked item disposed of. If your side did not claim

## The two closing passes

**Forward, from grounds to relief.** Walk each ground and name the request it feeds. A ground feeding
no request is either unfinished or should have been dropped at selection.

**Backward, from relief to grounds.** Walk each request and name the ground that supports it, and the
operative item it addresses. A request with no ground is an overreach; a request with no item is
misaddressed.

Run both. They fail in different ways and neither catches the other's failure. The forward pass
catches the ground you argued and forgot to ask for. The backward pass catches the request you
carried over from an earlier draft or from the pleading below.

## Figures agree in both directions

The figure in a request equals the figure its grounds produced. Where several grounds affect one
item, the request carries the total and each ground carries its own contribution, and the
contributions sum to the total. Check the sum; this is where drafts diverge from themselves.

## Where the document ends

After the relief and the signature block, a filing is over. Anything further is either already said
or does not belong to the reader. In particular, a filing does not end with a summary of its own
grounds, an appeal to the tribunal's diligence, an attachment list standing in for evidence not being
filed, or any note addressed to someone other than the tribunal.
