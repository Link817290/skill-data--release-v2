# Figures, counts, periods, and the order of events in a cross examination

Apply this before writing any sentence of the opinion that turns on a money figure, a count, a
period, or the sequence in which things happened. In a cross examination the figures that matter
most are usually the other side's own: they either fail to support the purpose offered, or they
concede part of your client's case.

## 1. Collect the figures before using one

From the figure and date inventory, list every figure that bears on the quantity in issue, with
the document each came from and who made that document: a contract figure, a figure in the other
side's statement or letter, a figure from an expert or administrative determination, a figure
someone computed for the filing. They are rarely equal.

Verification: for every figure you use, you can name its document and say whose document it is.

## 2. Subtract the other side's own figures

Check whether two figures in the other side's own documents bear on the same quantity (a total and
an amount paid, a settled sum and an amount it says is outstanding, a count claimed and a count
recorded).

- If subtracting one from the other shows that part of your client's claim is not in dispute, or
  that the other side's figure does not support its purpose, write the subtraction under the item
  it answers: the two figures, their documents, and the result, in the same sentence or the next.
- Adopt a figure from the other side's documents that favours your client; do not dispute it
  elsewhere.

Counterexample: when the deliverable is not about quantum, do not import a computation of your own
design. The exception is exactly this one: a difference between figures the other side's own
documents fix, which answers the purpose the item is offered for.

## 3. Write the derivation where the figure is used

A derived figure appears with the values it was derived from, in the same place: base, movement,
result. Where an amount is partly disputed, give the undisputed and the disputed parts separately.

## 4. Never assert a figure or count nothing supports

Every figure and count in the delivered text is either taken from a document in the inputs or
derived in the text from figures that are. Do not derive a count by setting one list against
another unless the documents themselves show how the two lists correspond. If the inputs do not fix
a number, do not state one; argue from what the documents do fix.

When a registration or schedule states a collateral value, compare it with the amount for which
the registration is offered. A mismatch is not proof that either figure is wrong; it is a limit on
using that record to prove that the disputed amount falls within the registered property. Treat an
illegible digit or unit as unresolved, not as a value you may normalize.

## 5. Order the events, then state the consequence

List the dated events that matter, each with the document that dates it. For every argument that
depends on time, say which event came first and what follows.

- A fact or a status that arose after a decision could not have been considered in it.
- An event inside a running period affects it; one outside does not. Give the start point, the
  events that affected it and the end point, then the conclusion.
- Where a condition had to be satisfied before an obligation arose, place the alleged satisfaction
  against the date of the obligation.
- Where a record covers one period and the fact in issue concerns another, say so as a mismatch.

Verification: each time based argument ends in a stated legal consequence, not in a chronology.

## 6. Self-check

1. Every figure, count and date traces to an input document or to a derivation written in the text.
2. Any subtraction of the other side's own figures that answers a purpose is written under the item.
3. No favourable figure is disputed, and no figure is taken from a document whose genuineness you
   rejected.
4. Nothing the inputs leave open has been filled in with a plausible value.

## 7. Stop conditions

- Do not build a table of figures or a chronology in the filing; the figures sit in the sentences
  that use them.
- If two documents give different values for the same quantity and nothing decides between them,
  rely on the part both support. Do not pick one silently, and do not ask for further material.
- Do not recompute a figure a document states directly and no one disputes.
