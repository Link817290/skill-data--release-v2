# Anatomy of the filing you are answering

Read this while building the grounds inventory. Each section says what that kind of filing must
establish, where its grounds hide, what your answer may and may not contain, and how to tell that the
decomposition is finished. Use the section that matches the filing in front of you; where the inputs
contain more than one opposing document, run the relevant sections in turn and keep one inventory.

## 1. A complaint

**What it must establish.** Every head of relief rests on a small set of elements: a relationship or
duty, a breach or a triggering event, a causal link, and a quantum. The claimant must carry all of
them; you only have to break one per head.

**Where the grounds hide.** The prayer tells you how many heads there are. The facts and reasons tell
you which elements the claimant thinks it has. The evidence list's proof purposes tell you which
elements it knows are weak, because that column is where a claimant states what it hopes each document
will do.

**Operation.**

1. Tabulate the heads of relief. For each, write the elements, then mark which of them the filing
   actually addresses and which it assumes.
2. Attack the assumed ones first. An element the claimant did not argue is an element it has not proved.
3. Separate liability from quantum for each head. "We owe nothing" and "we do not owe this much" are
   different answers with different consequences, and a head can fail on either.
4. Look for conditions attached to the obligation: a payment that depends on an event, an approval, a
   third party's confirmation, a document being issued. A condition that has not occurred defeats the
   head without any dispute about the underlying work.
5. Check whether a head is barred for a reason unrelated to its merits, and whether that reason is
   consistent with the rest of your answer (see the entry's step 5).

**Verification.** Every head of relief in the prayer appears in your inventory with at least one
element you say fails, and each failure is tied to something in the record.

**Counterexample.** Do not answer a head the claimant did not bring because the file suggests it could
have. That invites it.

## 2. A counterclaim

An answer to a counterclaim is still an answer. Everything in section 1 applies to the counterclaim's
own heads, with three additions.

- **You are the defendant on these heads, and only these.** Your own claim is already pleaded elsewhere
  and this document does not advance it. Arguments that your claim is good belong here only where they
  are the reason a counterclaim head fails.
- **Counterclaims in performance disputes concentrate on defects, delay and cost.** For each, the
  elements are: the event occurred, it is attributable to you, it caused the loss claimed, and the loss
  is the figure claimed. Attribution and causation are usually the weakest and the least argued.
- **Notice and time discipline cut both ways.** When a counterclaim rests on a defect or a delay, the
  record of who told whom, and when, is normally the decisive material: an objection raised long after
  the event, or after some other trigger, tells its own story. Order the correspondence before you
  characterise it.

**Verification.** Each counterclaim head has an element you deny, and the denial rests on the record
rather than on the strength of your own claim.

## 3. A jurisdiction, forum or admissibility objection

**What it must establish.** That this tribunal should not hear this case at all, on a ground that can
be decided without trying the merits.

**Operation.**

1. Classify every point in the objection as procedural or substantive. Procedural points you answer.
   Substantive points you name, and say expressly that they are for the merits stage after evidence.
2. Having said that, do not then decide any of them. This is the most common self-inflicted wound in
   this posture: a sentence deferring the issue, followed within a page by a sentence resolving it.
3. Answer the procedural points with the rule that governs the route into court: which tribunal the
   subject matter belongs to, what makes a clause binding on a person who did not sign it, what effect
   an earlier disposal has, what a change of party or a change of right does to any of that.
4. Where the objection relies on an earlier ruling, place the events in time. Something that happened
   after the earlier disposal is by definition not something the earlier disposal decided.
5. Close on consequence: if the objection succeeded, where would the claim go? Showing that it would go
   nowhere - that no other forum is open to this party on this right - is a complete answer in itself and
   is routinely left out.

**Verification.** Every point of the objection is classified; no substantive point is resolved in your
text; the answer ends with what happens if the objection is upheld.

**Counterexample.** If the instruction or the posture asks you to argue the merits as well, do it in a
separate, labelled part, and keep the deferral language out of that part.

## 4. An appeal

**What it must establish.** That the decision below is wrong in a way that the appellate court can and
should correct: a fact wrongly found, evidence wrongly assessed, law wrongly applied, or a procedure
that vitiates the result.

**Operation.**

1. Split the appeal into its grounds and label each with which of those four kinds it is. A ground
   framed as a legal error often turns out to be an attack on a finding of fact, which is a different
   and usually harder thing for the appellant.
2. For each ground, go back to the decision and find what it actually held and on what material. Then
   answer with the decision's own reasoning where it helps you, naming the holding you are supporting.
3. Where the appellant offers an alternative rule, check what that rule presupposes before you argue
   about its content. Alternative and residual rules almost always apply only in the absence of
   something; showing that the something is present ends the ground (see
   [rebuttal-construction.md](rebuttal-construction.md) section 2).
4. Where the ground is procedural, answer it on its own terms: the step was lawful, and separately, it
   caused no prejudice.
5. Read the appeal for admissions. An appellant explaining why it could not produce something, or
   describing its own conduct, hands you a fact you no longer have to prove.

**Verification.** Each ground has an answer of the same kind as the ground; no ground is answered only
by asserting that the decision is right.

## 5. A retrial petition

**What it must establish.** Two things, and they are separable. First, that the petition fits one of the
limited grounds on which a concluded case may be reopened at all. Second, that on the merits the
decision was wrong.

**Operation.**

1. Answer at both levels for each ground: the ground is not made out on its own terms, and in any event
   it is wrong. Skipping the first level throws away the easier argument.
2. Where the petition relies on material said to be new, test it against what "new" requires: material
   that existed and could have been obtained before is not new merely because it has been reworked into
   a fresh document since.
3. Where the petition asks for something that was never asked for in the original proceedings, say so.
   The reopened case is bounded by the original claims.
4. Where the petition alleges a procedural defect, check the record for what actually happened, and
   answer with the specific act - who was appointed, who took part, what was put to whom - rather than
   with a general assertion of regularity.
5. Decide your own stance on the decision under attack before you defend it: see
   [instance-and-review-scope.md](instance-and-review-scope.md) section 3. Resisting the petition does
   not require you to declare the decision perfect, and declaring it perfect can cost you a right of
   your own.

**Verification.** For each ground of the petition you have both an admissibility answer and a merits
answer, and neither of them endorses more of the decision than you need.

## 6. An evidence bundle acting as the filing

Sometimes the only thing the other side has put in is a bundle with a list. The list is still a set of
assertions: the proof purpose column states, for each item, the proposition its producer wants drawn
from it.

**Operation.** Treat each proof purpose as a ground. For each, decide whether the item reaches the
proposition, and answer the gap rather than the document. Where a proof purpose is not supported by any
item in the bundle, say that the proposition is unsupported rather than arguing about admissibility.

**Verification.** Every proof purpose has a response. A response that only repeats the item's name and
attaches a label is not a response.

**Counterexample.** Where the instruction asks for a pleading and not for an opinion on evidence, keep
this analysis inside the grounds it supports; a full item by item treatment of the bundle is a different
deliverable.

## Stop conditions for this reference

- If the filing in front of you does not match any section here, decompose it by the same method: what
  must this filing establish, what has it actually argued, and which element is unargued.
- If two of these sections both apply because the inputs contain two opposing filings, keep the roles
  separate in the text. Answering an appeal and a counterclaim in one undifferentiated block produces
  the role contradictions listed in the entry's step 5.
