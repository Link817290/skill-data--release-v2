# Instance, review scope and what you may safely assert

Read this after the grounds inventory and before you fix stances, whenever the case has already been
decided once, or the stage you are in limits what may be decided. The question this reference answers
is narrow and it is the one that decides most self-inflicted losses: given where the case is and who
you act for, what am I allowed to say, and what will saying it cost me?

## 1. First instance

Everything is open, and the record is only what the parties have put in.

- Your answer may deny anything the other side has not proved. It may not assume facts that would need
  evidence you do not have.
- Where a fact is in the file but nobody has drawn the conclusion from it, that conclusion is yours to
  make, and it is worth more than a repetition of what the file says.
- Take care with points that presuppose the opposite of your main defence. A defence that is only
  available on the footing that the claim has accrued sits badly beside a defence that it has not.
  Either subordinate one to the other with an explicit alternative frame, or drop it.

**Verification.** Every denial is a denial of something the other side actually has to prove, and every
affirmative defence is compatible with the others (entry step 5).

## 2. Second instance, answering an appeal

**The shape of the decision below matters more than its rhetoric.** Before deciding your stance, write
out what the decision actually ordered, item by item, and mark each item as in your favour, against you,
or mixed. Also mark how the costs of the stage below were divided.

Rules that follow:

- **Your request must name a scope and keep it.** Asking for the whole decision to be upheld and asking
  for one of its items to be upheld are different requests. Pick the one that matches the marking you
  just made, and use it everywhere: in the opening, in the reasoning and in the closing request.
- **If you did not appeal, you cannot improve your position** through this document. Asking for an item
  that went against you to be changed, or for the costs of the stage below to be reallocated, is asking
  for relief you have not claimed. It also signals that you are not content with the decision you are
  asking the court to uphold.
- **Endorse the holdings you need, by name.** "The finding on the payment condition is correct because
  it rests on the clause and on the confirmation record" is worth something. "The judgment is correct in
  its findings, its evidence and its law" gives away every part of it you dislike, and it will be quoted
  back at you.
- **Where the decision is against you on a point you accept losing**, be silent rather than
  affirmative. Silence is not a concession; an endorsement is.
- **Costs of this stage are a separate matter** from the allocation made below. Deal with this stage and
  leave the earlier allocation alone unless a ground requires otherwise.

**Verification.** Read your closing request and your opening paragraph side by side. Do they name the
same scope? Then search the draft for every sentence that approves the decision and check that each one
is restricted to a named holding.

**Counterexample.** Where the appellant's ground is that the whole decision is wrong and your answer
really is that the whole of it is right, say so once, and still name the holdings on the contested
grounds.

## 3. Retrial, answering a petition against a decision that is partly against you

This is the posture that most often produces a document arguing against the party who wrote it.

- **Resisting a petition does not require defending the decision.** The petition fails if it does not
  meet the conditions for reopening, whatever the merits. Run that argument first and keep it separate.
- **Never endorse the decision in unrestricted terms** when you have your own grievance against it,
  and especially not by reciting a general formula of correctness covering findings, evidence, law and
  procedure at once. If you elsewhere reserve your disagreement with part of that decision - or if the
  record shows your side arguing against part of it - the unrestricted endorsement contradicts your own
  reservation, and it forfeits the grounds you might one day need.
- **A procedural flaw that is real and on the record may be worth conceding** rather than denying,
  particularly where the flaw runs against you too. Denying an irregularity that the file records
  destroys your credibility on the grounds where you are right, and it abandons the ground for your own
  side.
- **Know which errors run in which direction.** Where a decision erred in a way that favours the
  petitioner, saying so is part of your answer. Where it erred in a way that favours you, you do not
  have to say anything about it, but you must not describe it as correct.
- **The scope of a reopened case is bounded by the original claims.** A request that was never made
  before cannot be made now, and this is an answer to the request rather than to its merits.

**Verification.** List every sentence in your draft that says something positive about the decision
under attack. For each, name the specific holding it covers and check that it is a holding you want.
Then list every reservation you make about the same decision. The two lists must not overlap.

## 4. A stage that decides only one question

Objections to jurisdiction, forum, admissibility or the route into court are decided before and
separately from the merits. The discipline is stated in
[opposing-filing-anatomy.md](opposing-filing-anatomy.md) section 3; the reason it belongs here is that
it is a scope of review rule, not a drafting preference.

- Say which of the other side's points need evidence and a hearing, and leave them there.
- Having deferred an issue, do not decide it. Stating a rule that resolves the deferred issue is
  deciding it, even in a sentence that looks like background.
- Do not import merits material as support for the procedural answer unless it is the procedural answer.

**Verification.** After drafting, find every sentence that states a conclusion about a deferred issue.
There should be none.

## 5. Changes of party and derived rights

Where the right in issue reached your client from someone else, or where the other side says it did,
the instance analysis has an extra layer.

- Separate the transfer from the underlying right. Objections to how a right moved do not usually touch
  whether the right exists, and vice versa.
- Work out what moves with the right and what does not: which defences the obligor keeps, which
  agreements between the original parties bind the new holder, what has to be done to make the change
  effective against the obligor, and whether the obligor's agreement was needed at all.
- The internal arrangements between the transferor and third parties are normally beside the point.
  Saying so, in terms, is an answer; ignoring them lets the other side's framing stand.
- Where the other side says the right is too uncertain to have moved, answer at two levels: uncertainty
  in amount does not prevent a right from existing or moving, and the amount can be fixed later by the
  procedure that exists for fixing it.

**Verification.** Each of the other side's objections to the transfer is met either by the rule about
what moves or by the rule about what the obligor keeps, and neither answer concedes the other.

## Stop conditions

- If you cannot tell from the inputs what the decision below actually ordered, say so and answer the
  grounds on the material you have, rather than assuming an operative part.
- If your instructions and the record point to different stances - the file shows your side arguing one
  way and the instruction puts you on the other side of it - follow the party you are told to act for and
  flag the inconsistency; do not silently write for both.
