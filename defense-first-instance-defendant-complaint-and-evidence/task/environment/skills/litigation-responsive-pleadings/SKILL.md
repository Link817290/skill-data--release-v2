# Responsive pleadings in civil litigation

The reader is the judge who already has the opposing filing in front of them. They do not need it
described back. They need, for every ground the other side takes, a reason to reject it that they can
put in a judgment, and a clear statement of what you want the court to do.

## What this deliverable is, and what it is not

It answers the other side's grounds one at a time, for one named party, and it ends in relief the
tribunal can grant.

It is not a narrative of the case, not a brief on the law in general, not a claim of your own, and not
a rehearsal of the hearing. Three consequences follow, and they are the ones most often ignored.

- **You answer, you do not claim.** A responsive pleading cannot introduce relief your side has not
  claimed in the proceedings. Neither can it introduce a new cause of action under the cover of an
  argument. Reserving the right to bring something separately is not the same as asking for it here.
- **Write only what the posture calls for.** A section nobody asked for displaces the analysis that
  was asked for, and it is where invented facts get in.
- **Copying is not answering.** Reproducing the opposing filing's narrative, the evidence list's
  columns, or a judgment's own reasoning adds nothing the tribunal does not have. What counts is the
  proposition you assert and the reason you give for it.

Counterexample: where the posture genuinely calls for an additional element - a costs position, a
procedural request the rules require you to make now - write it, keep it in its own place, and do not
let it absorb the ground by ground analysis.

## Step 1: build the grounds inventory before drafting anything

One row per independent ground the other side advances. Harvest them from every input, not just the
obvious one:

- the numbered grounds and the prayer of the opposing filing;
- its facts and reasons narrative, where one paragraph routinely carries several independent grounds;
- the proof purposes stated against each item of its evidence list, each of which is an assertion;
- for an answer at a higher instance, each finding of the decision below that the other side attacks,
  and each statutory ground it invokes;
- findings in the record that are adverse to you and that the tribunal will have to deal with whether
  or not the other side raised them.

Split compound grounds. Give each row: the ground as the other side puts it, what that ground must
establish in order to succeed, your stance (step 2), the place in your draft where it is answered, and
what in the record supports your answer.

Verification: every row has a place in the draft, or a recorded reason why no answer is owed at this
stage. A row with neither is a ground you are about to leave standing, and an unanswered ground is the
single most common way these pleadings lose their value.

Counterexample: do not invent grounds the other side has not taken in order to knock them down. If the
inputs contain only the bundle and no filing, the inventory comes from the proof purposes alone.

## Step 2: fix your stance on each ground before you write a sentence

Four stances, and the choice is a decision, not a reflex:

| Stance | Use it when | What it commits you to |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Deny | the record does not support the fact or the proposition | you must say what the record shows instead |
| Distinguish or limit | the material is real but does not reach the conclusion drawn from it | you must say where it stops and why |
| Concede with its consequence | the point is true, costs you nothing, and buys credibility | you must state the consequence you accept |
| Defer | the point belongs to a different or later stage of the proceedings | you must not then decide it yourself |

Rules that decide the stance:

- **Test every adoption against your own client's position.** Before you assert anything about a
  document, a figure, a finding or a procedural act, ask what your side loses if the tribunal holds you
  to it. Endorsing a finding that is adverse to you, accepting the other side's computation, or
  affirming in general terms a decision you elsewhere disagree with, all give away ground for nothing.
- **Endorse narrowly.** When a decision or a document is partly in your favour and partly against you,
  endorse the specific holding you rely on, named as such, and stay silent on the rest. A blanket
  approval of the whole instrument absorbs the parts that hurt you.
- **Deferral is a decision not to decide.** If you have told the tribunal that an issue needs a later
  stage, you cannot resolve that same issue a few lines further down.
- **One stance per proposition** across the whole document.

Verification: for each row of the inventory you can name the stance in one word and say in one clause
what it costs you. If the answer is "it costs me the very thing I am defending", the stance is wrong.

Counterexample: a concession is not a defect. Conceding a procedural irregularity that you intend to
rely on, or a figure you will not dispute, is often the strongest move available. What is never
available is conceding the proposition your own case depends on.

## Step 3: make each answer complete inside one local span

Under the heading for that ground, one passage must carry three things together: the object it is
about, the operative proposition you assert, and the ground for that proposition. A conclusion is read
where it sits.

These are the incomplete forms that look finished and are not:

- a conclusion with no computation beside it - a period is called expired but the expiry is never
  worked out, a figure is called wrong but the correct one is never given;
- a fact with no legal consequence attached - two dates are contrasted and nothing is drawn from the
  contrast;
- a rule recited with no application - the standard appears but the case's own facts are never run
  through it;
- one limb of a two limb move - the burden is allocated but the failure to discharge it is not stated,
  a document is called unilateral but what it therefore cannot prove is not;
- an answer parked in the opening summary, in a table, or in the closing paragraph instead of under the
  ground it answers.

Verification: cover the whole document except one ground's section and read that section alone. Object,
proposition and ground must all still be there. Then do it for the next ground.

Counterexample: a proposition that really is about the case as a whole belongs in one place; repeating
it under every ground is padding, and padding is what pushes these documents past the length the
tribunal expects.

## Step 4: every assertion carries a location in the record

Before an affirmative sentence goes in, name where in the inputs it comes from: which document, which
clause, which entry, which line of the decision. Do this while drafting, not afterwards.

Three kinds of sentence fail this test and read as your own case even though nothing supports them:

- **Acts.** That your side filed a named exhibit, gave a notice, made an objection; that the court
  below organised a step, called a witness, obtained an answer; that a party did or did not do
  something at a hearing. If the record does not record the act, you are the source of it.
- **Stances of other people.** That a party or a non-party raised no objection, accepted a proposal,
  agreed that value had been exchanged, or acquiesced in something. Silence in the record is not
  evidence of assent, and an absent party's absence is not its agreement.
- **Terms, rules and numbers.** A contractual requirement stated as if it were in the contract, a rate
  or benchmark figure stated as if it were in the file, a practice of the courts stated as if it were a
  rule. Watch particularly for a real term attached to the wrong object: the act may exist in the
  contract while the thing it governs is something else entirely.

A claim that a named source is silent about something is itself an assertion about that source. Check
the source before you make it; a source that does record the thing turns the claim into a false
statement about the evidence.

The lawful ways to make the same point:

- say that the other side has not proved the fact - allocating a burden asserts nothing about the world;
- attribute the figure or the statement to whoever produced it, and argue about it as theirs;
- ask the tribunal to determine the point by the procedure available for it;
- state the inference, label it as an inference, and name the recorded facts it rests on;
- mark the point as not determinable on the inputs and say what would be needed to determine it.

Verification: for every affirmative sentence about an act, a stance, a term or a number, you can point
at the place it came from. Where you cannot, rewrite it into one of the five forms above, or delete it.

Counterexample: an inference you draw openly from recorded facts is argument, not an invention. The
defect is the unsignalled leap, not the reasoning.

## Step 5: sweep the finished draft for contradictions

Before delivering, list every proposition you asserted about each of these, then look for pairs that
cannot both be true:

- each contested figure and each date;
- the validity, effect and scope of each document;
- each party's conduct and each procedural act;
- the relief you ask for.

Pairs that recur in this kind of document:

- you performed an obligation, and the obligation had not fallen due;
- an unconditional limitation defence, and a period that has not begun to run;
- a decision is correct in general terms, and you reserve your disagreement with part of it;
- part of a decision should stand, and the whole of it should stand;
- a party is in one procedural role in the opening, and in the opposite role later;
- a chronology you assert, against dates you state elsewhere in the same document.

Where two assertions conflict, drop one. Hedging both leaves both in the document. The one legitimate
way to hold incompatible positions is an alternative frame carried on the sentence itself ("even if the
tribunal were to find X, then Y"), and it works only where the frame is actually there.

Verification: read the draft once for figures and dates only, once for stances on documents, once for
procedural roles and acts. Each pass is quick because you are looking for one thing.

## Step 6: close into the relief

The closing request disposes of the opposing filing and nothing else.

- It matches the grounds you argued: every ground you won should be visible in what you ask for, and
  you should not ask for anything no ground supports.
- Ask for exactly the scope you want. Wanting the whole of a decision to stand and wanting one part of
  it to stand are different requests; they cannot both appear.
- Where your own arguments produce a fallback, state it as an alternative, in order, and keep it
  consistent with the primary request.
- Deal with costs for the stage you are in, and do not disturb an allocation made at an earlier stage
  unless a ground you argued asks for that.
- Nothing appears here that your side has not claimed in the proceedings.

## Self-check before delivering

1. Does every row of the grounds inventory have an answer in the delivered text, or a recorded reason
   why none is owed at this stage?
2. Is the stance on each ground the one that serves the party you act for, and is it the same stance
   everywhere that proposition appears?
3. Does each answer carry its object, its proposition and its ground inside one span, under the heading
   for that ground?
4. Is every figure either quoted with its source or derived in place, and every period closed with its
   computed endpoint and its consequence?
5. Can you point to the place in the record behind every affirmative statement about an act, a stance,
   a term or a number, with the rest rewritten as burden statements, attributions, marked inferences or
   marked gaps?
6. Did the contradiction passes of step 5 come back clean?
7. Does the relief dispose of the opposing filing, match the grounds argued, and contain nothing your
   side has not claimed?
8. Have you removed everything the posture did not call for, including restatement of the inputs?

## Failure modes and stop conditions

- **Narrating instead of answering.** A chronology of the project or the proceedings is not a ground.
  If a paragraph would be equally at home in either side's document, it is not doing work.
- **Adopting the other side's framing.** Answering inside the opponent's categories concedes the
  categories. Where the right answer is that the issue is differently shaped, say so first and then
  answer.
- **Arguing the merits from a procedural posture.** When the filing responds to an objection about
  jurisdiction, forum or admissibility, classify each of the other side's points as procedural or
  substantive and say that the substantive ones belong to the merits stage. Do not decide them.
- **Self-harming generosity.** Approving a decision, an appraisal or a computation in blanket terms
  because it is mostly in your favour costs you the parts that are not.
- **Filling gaps with plausible detail.** Where the inputs do not fix a name, a date, a case number, an
  identifier or a figure, leave it blank or mark it. A plausible invention is the one error a tribunal
  can verify against the file in seconds.
- **Stop and mark.** If the inputs do not let you decide a ground, say what would be needed and mark it
  undetermined. Do not assert a conclusion you cannot support in order to fill the row.
