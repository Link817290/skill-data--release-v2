# From screenplay to a shot plan

## Extract obligations before designing shots

Read the whole story before assigning durations. The ending often determines which expectation must be established early. Extract the following into a small working table; merge columns when the script is simple.

| Field | Decision it supports |
| --- | --- |
| Narrative fact and dialogue meaning | What must the audience learn, even if wording is lightly edited? |
| Speaker and viewpoint | Is this spoken interaction, a message, private thought, or an imagined scene? |
| Visible action or state | What must actually appear or change on screen? |
| Attitude and intensity | Is the performance tentative, confident, mocking, startled, or resigned, and how strongly? |
| Dependency | What must already be understood, and what later beat depends on this one? |
| Planned evidence | Which performance, object, graphic, or environmental change will communicate it? |

Keep explicit requirements distinct from your interpretation. If the script specifies a gesture, posture, or display, preserve it; if it leaves the camera or transition open, choose freely. Resolve ambiguous presentation through the surrounding story rather than adding new plot facts.

For example, a character describing a task as easy conveys information. Their relaxed posture conveys confidence. A subsequent expanding task list changes the audience's understanding. These are separate screen events even if they share one shot. Showing only the dialogue loses the contrast; showing only a startled reaction loses its cause.

## Track physical roles and private reactions

Maintain separate entries for physical characters and thought performances. Record who a thought belongs to and what triggered it. Use a repeated label, framing treatment, or immediate edit relationship to establish that convention. A reaction performer need not look identical to the physical character when it is clearly a private cutaway; unexplained swaps inside the physical scene create a different continuity problem.

Create an emotional progression from the actual script. Compare adjacent reactions, especially when both might be loosely called surprise or sadness. Specify what becomes stronger, weaker, or different. Do not select an extreme reaction for an early mild beat merely because it is more recognizable.

## Turn narrative beats into shots

Choose the shot's job before its duration: establish a situation, deliver information, expose a misunderstanding, show a response, compress repeated activity, or pay off an earlier expectation. Combine beats when one composition communicates them clearly. Split a beat when the viewer needs to shift attention from information to a face or gesture.

For each planned shot, record a useful subset of:

- Timeline range and narrative purpose.
- Physical role or thought owner, performer alias, source range, and important action moment.
- Background and relevant state entering and leaving the shot.
- Caption meaning, story graphic, and their reveal intervals.
- Sound cue and the cut or hold that ends the shot.

Use semantic aliases internally and retain a mapping to actual source files. The final edit plan should describe the completed film using the brief's required schema; this working model need not become a large custom schema.

## Design state changes, not decorative counters

For recurring elements, write down the initial state, trigger, and next state. Examples include a message becoming readable, a list accumulating, a task being revised, or lighting changing as time passes. Couple related signals: a time jump should agree with environment and activity; a reset in a progress display should correspond to a new attempt rather than accidental animation looping.

A useful activity montage keeps the worker recognizable while changing evidence around them. Advancing time, evolving save labels, and shifting light can communicate duration and repetition when the screenplay calls for them. Props support this context but do not replace the required activity. Use only the devices justified by the brief and story.

Treat oversized words, request boards, and progress graphics as part of the dramatic action. Define what they reveal, when they change, and how long they remain legible. Give the most consequential information the strongest emphasis. An impact word can intentionally sit behind a performer if its meaning and the performance remain readable.

## Allocate time and check coverage

Reserve time for essential reading and the payoff, then distribute the remaining time by dramatic importance. Preview captions at delivery size before locking timings. Trim redundant holds or transitions when the budget is tight; preserve causal information and distinct emotional beats.

Review the draft once with captions hidden: actions, relationships, and major changes should still be visible. Review dialogue separately for meaning and speaker attribution. These are complementary checks, not a requirement that every line be understandable without words.

Finally, map each screenplay obligation to an interval in the exported video. Inspect both the state before a change and the state after it. A single attractive still cannot prove an accumulating list, moving progress, or a change in attitude. Update the working plan when the edit changes rather than letting it describe an abandoned version.
