# Casting and usable performances

## Inspect a shortlist, then inspect motion

Start from the screenplay's role and action requirements. Use metadata and names to shortlist likely candidates from the supplied pool; avoid exhaustive playback of a large source library. Probe duration, frame rate, dimensions, audio, and transparency. Generate a few labeled thumbnails across each shortlisted clip to identify posture, embedded objects, and broad expression.

A thumbnail is only a first pass. Inspect the usable action range for plausible candidates: anticipation, gesture or expression onset, peak, and recovery. Expand sampling or play the segment when those moments are unclear. Check sound separately; the presence of an audio stream does not establish useful audio. If the shortlist lacks a match, inspect the next plausible candidates instead of forcing a convenient clip into the role.

## Compare against the complete performance requirement

Use a compact casting note for each role or thought beat:

| Dimension | What to inspect |
| --- | --- |
| Activity and posture | Does the subject perform the required action with the intended body position? |
| Interaction | Is the gesture directed toward another character, an object, or the viewer? |
| Attitude | Does the expression communicate the intended social or emotional meaning? |
| Intensity and trajectory | Does the expression fit this point in the story, including its onset and recovery? |
| Embedded setting | Do the source's desk, chair, hands, or other objects support the intended scene? |
| Usable range | Can the action be staged and held long enough without an incompatible reset? |

Treat explicit behavioral requirements as gates before comparing image quality or ease of keying. For a task requiring a seated desktop action, generic rapid paw motion may not supply the same action. For a mocking interaction, a laugh without the relevant directed gesture may not carry the intended relationship. A label cannot supply an absent posture, gesture, or expression.

Keep the reason for selecting a clip and, when close candidates exist, the decisive reason for rejecting alternatives. This prevents later cleanup from silently substituting a different performance. Notes should refer to observable behavior rather than inventing personality from appearance.

## Maintain continuity deliberately

Reuse the selected performer for a recurring physical role. Distinguish an alternate trim or crop of the same performance from a different actor with a similar emotion. An alternate source is useful only if it preserves both identity and the required action. When revising, check all occurrences of the affected role, not only the replaced shot.

Internal reactions may use distinct performers when the edit clearly assigns them to the same viewpoint. Preserve the progression between those reactions; do not reuse one convenient expression for every emotional intensity. A performer serving another physical role can also create unintended ambiguity if reused as a private thought.

## Fit the source range to the shot

Select the action range before stretching the clip to fill the timeline. Position the significant gesture or expression relative to the reveal. Trim a long setup if it delays comprehension; preserve enough anticipation when it makes the movement intelligible.

Use a loop for sustained repetitive action only when the boundary is acceptable. A one-time point, startle, or head turn often needs a trim and hold instead. Use a freeze deliberately for a stunned pause or ending; verify that its chosen frame carries the expression. Speed changes can strengthen repetitive activity but may distort a conversational gesture or its sound.

Check the final composite in motion. Cropping can remove the very gesture that justified selection, and downscaling can make an expression unreadable. Reposition, scale, or simplify the surrounding composition before replacing a correctly matched performance.
