# Digital items, estimates, and related contract costs

Read this reference when consideration is paid before a digital item is selected, when items have different consumption patterns, or when processing costs are deferred.

## Contract inception and deferred consideration

Separate these events:

1. cash or stored-value purchase;
2. the customer's ability to select an item;
3. item acquisition;
4. item use or consumption;
5. expiration, breakage, or customer inactivity.

For each event, identify what enforceable promise has transferred. Evaluate the accounting event and materiality separately. Event-timing data can quantify the size of a difference, while the contract and transfer of the promise determine which event is the recognition point.

## Classifying digital items

Start from how benefit is consumed, not from product labels.

An item commonly has a consumption-type pattern when a customer action exhausts its benefit and the item no longer remains available. An item commonly has a continuing-access pattern when the item persists and the entity must keep it available for use, display, or interaction.

Test edge cases:

- an item persists visually but its functional benefit is exhausted;
- access is technically indefinite but depends on continued platform service;
- an item can be used across several experiences but not transferred outside the platform;
- an item is removed by the entity before customer inactivity; or
- a subscription grants both stored value and period-specific rights.

The population may require more than two classes. Use the classes supported by the contract and usage evidence rather than forcing an industry label.

## Unclassified population

When not every transaction is tagged, define the known and unknown populations. Test whether the known population is representative of the unknown one.

Required evidence normally includes:

- a complete reconciliation from transaction events to the classified and unclassified populations;
- stable definitions and change control for classification rules;
- sampling or back-testing of automated classifications;
- comparison of user behavior, product mix, and experience type between populations;
- a documented estimation technique and uncertainty range; and
- review controls over changes in the estimate.

Do not extrapolate from a convenient subset without testing selection bias. If the unknown population is concentrated in different products or users, an estimate based on the known group may not be representative.

## Service-period estimate

For a continuing-access item, an estimated service period can be based on customer activity only when customer inactivity reasonably represents the end of the item's availability or benefit. Audit the estimate as a model:

- define the start and end events;
- evaluate censored cohorts and recent cohort maturity;
- test data completeness and user identity changes;
- compare historical estimates with subsequent retention;
- test sensitivity to cohort weighting and inactivity thresholds; and
- determine whether a change is an estimate update, an error correction, or a change caused by a revised accounting unit.

An estimate derived cleanly from the wrong accounting unit is still wrong.

## Contract-cost linkage

Map each deferred cost pool to the revenue population it supports. Confirm that the cost is incremental to obtaining the contract and expected to be recovered. Then amortize it consistently with the transfer of the related goods or services.

If renewal or later-sale costs are commensurate with initial costs, the initial cost generally should not be stretched merely to cover anticipated renewals. “Commensurate” concerns the economic relationship, not exact equality. Test actual rate schedules and material deviations.

When the revenue population contains several transfer patterns, test whether the cost pool can be attributed directly. If it cannot, evaluate whether a systematic allocation faithfully represents the related transfers. Reconcile the amortization model to both the deferred-cost balance and recognized expense.

## Subscription bundles

For a subscription that includes stored value and other rights, inventory every promise before applying materiality. Assess whether each promise is capable of being distinct and separately identifiable. If management concludes a promise is immaterial, obtain:

- a standalone-value analysis;
- an allocation sensitivity;
- current-period and deferred-revenue effects;
- qualitative considerations; and
- evidence that cancellation terms are reflected in the enforceable contract period.

Conclude separately on accounting, measurement, and disclosure. For each conclusion, identify whether the evidence comes from contract terms, an allocation analysis, recorded amounts, or the issued note.
