# Evidence Hierarchy and Inclusion Boundaries

## 1. Evidence ledger

First record, by case, the detail-row ID, source department or item, original text, evidence level, candidate core finding, qualifiers, certainty, conflicts, and reason for inclusion. Do not write the final answer directly while reading.

## 2. Evidence levels

1. **Explicit classification or conclusion-bearing item**: the source record already provides a classification, conclusion, or explicit specialty finding. Preserve its meaning and wording whenever possible.
2. **Descriptive evidence with a single stable meaning**: an imaging or specialty description reliably expresses one finding but must be compressed into an atomic conclusion. Compress only when the medical meaning remains unchanged. Imaging departments describe many findings through a settled vocabulary in which the description and the named finding are two ways of writing the same thing; rendering such a description as its established name is compression, not escalation. The test is whether a reader of that modality could reach a different named finding from the same description — if yes, it is escalation and stays at this level as a description.
3. **Quantitative record read out by the item's own rule**: when a single indicator is read out by a fixed rule that belongs to the examination item itself and yields a named standard conclusion, that conclusion sits at level 1 for inclusion purposes even though the source cell holds only a number. When the number instead needs to be combined with other indicators, with history, or with a threshold the reader supplies from general knowledge, it stays a review clue and does not independently produce a diagnosis.
4. **Isolated laboratory deviation**: arrows, asterisks, and slight deviations from reference ranges are generally only clues and do not automatically become doctor-reviewed final conclusions.

“Medically abnormal” and “included in the doctor's final review” are different concepts. This task requires reconstruction of the latter.

## 3. Sufficient support

Evidence must support both the core finding and every qualifier in the conclusion. If a row supports only an organ abnormality, do not use it to add laterality, quantity, degree, characteristics, or certainty. Multiple evidence rows may jointly support one conclusion, but the role of each row must be explainable.

## 4. Exclusions and conflicts

- Explicit negatives such as normal, negative, no abnormality, or no abnormality found do not generate abnormal conclusions.
- An examination not performed or an unclear image only indicates missing evidence; it can produce neither an abnormal conclusion nor an inference of normality.
- When an explicit classification conflicts with a raw value, prefer the explicit classification closer to the final review; do not regrade it using a threshold you supplied yourself. This is a conflict rule and does not narrow level 3: where an item's own standing-readout rule is the only classification present, applying it is not regrading.
- A general statement that an organ is normal does not automatically override an explicit focal finding in the same sentence or another higher-level row.
- When two pieces of evidence disagree on laterality, quantity, degree, characteristics, or certainty, do not combine them into a stronger conclusion. Retain the higher-level evidence and record the conflict in the explanation when necessary.

## 5. Conservative boundary

Exclude a candidate if it can be supported only by general medical knowledge, an isolated value with no standing-readout rule, or an ambiguous description, and the materials contain neither a conclusion-bearing item nor a stable single meaning. Do not lower the threshold merely to give every case an abnormal conclusion.

The boundary being drawn here is between reading and reasoning. Reading out what an examination item already says — through its settled descriptive vocabulary or its own readout rule — is reconstruction, and belongs in the final review. Reasoning past what the item says, to reach a finding the examination did not itself express, is escalation and does not. Conservatism means refusing the second, not refusing the first; a review that drops findings the source materials plainly recorded is as wrong as one that invents findings they did not.

Multiple pieces of low-level evidence do not naturally become high-level evidence merely because there are more of them. In particular, do not independently combine multiple abnormal values from the same test group into a disease name. Unless the source materials contain a separate textual classification or conclusion-bearing item, continue to treat them as conditional candidates.
